# AI unit economics: can the capex pay back?

*Last updated: 2026-10-18 · Author: Claude (Fable 5.1 subagent) · Status: reviewed · Reviewer: Codex (gpt-6-astra) cross-check, applied 2026-10-17*

## Summary

- Big-four capex totals of $230.9bn and $380.5bn use company-specific definitions and Microsoft's June fiscal years; they are not calendar-year AI-only cohorts. Updated 2026 guidance sums to a $732.5bn midpoint, versus $747.5bn retaining Microsoft's earlier lease-accounting basis. Oracle's fiscal-year and quarterly spending cannot simply be added to calendar-year guidance (claims 1, 3, 5–8, 52).
- Published required-revenue estimates measure different layers, periods and returns. Our illustrative $750bn new cohort requires $295–468bn annually at a 50% owner EBITDA margin and 10% discount rate, depending on a six- or three-year equipment life. An exactly $1.36tn hypothetical new cohort requires $536–848bn; this is not a valuation of previously deployed cohorts (claims 11–14, 53).
- Revenue growth is real: Google Cloud grew 82% to $24.8bn in Q2 2026, and Microsoft disclosed an AI run-rate above $37bn in April. Reuters reports Anthropic's Q3 revenue of $16.8bn and adjusted profitability, alongside separate reporting of substantial historical losses and future compute commitments. Private financial reports remain unverified; overlapping revenue measures do not establish a sector-wide payback gap (claims 30, 33–34, 44–47).
- GPU economic life, utilization, operating margin and customer funding are major uncertainties. The illustrative $500bn short-lived pool has $83bn more annual depreciation at three years than at six. Server accounting schedules and rental listings do not establish GPU lifetime returns, and this sensitivity does not verify Burry's company-specific earnings claims (claims 17–24, 53).
- Prices move in both directions: some premium tiers and scheduled Google prices rise, while Sol and Opus prices fall; September's Opus 5.5 input/output cut was 20%. Google's aggregate token growth spans all surfaces, not just paid APIs. Rising usage alone establishes neither rising paid revenue nor a causal Jevons effect (claims 25–30, 48, 57).
- Epoch estimates final released-model runs at 9.6%, 22.6% and 12.3% of R&D compute spending at OpenAI, MiniMax and Z.ai. These are modeled ratios, not direct prospectus disclosures; experiments and unreleased models also involve training. Its historical 2.4x annual run-cost trend would imply billion-dollar runs by 2027 if continued. Accounting depends on ownership, rental, prepayment and lease terms (claims 36–38, 41–45).
- Financing and profitability differ by layer. Nvidia is highly profitable; CoreWeave has positive adjusted EBITDA but material losses after depreciation and financing. Customer advances, supplier credit and new securities fund the build and allocate risk. Anthropic's reported recent adjusted profit does not establish durable free cash flow under its future commitments (claims 42–47, 49–50, 55).

## Analysis

### 1. The capex payback math

Historical big-four totals are $230.9bn and $380.5bn on mixed periods and bases: Microsoft's fiscal years end in June, and the companies differ in their inclusion of leases and gross versus net purchases (claims 1–7). Latest 2026 guidance gives a $732.5bn midpoint, or $747.5bn keeping Microsoft's earlier lease-accounting basis (claim 52). Microsoft's building-life extension changes reported capex through lease classification without changing investment expectations (claim 41). Oracle's FY26 $55.7bn and Q1 FY27 $28.5bn are different periods; neither can be silently added to calendar-year forecasts. Goldman's ~$800bn is a scale comparison, not a reconciliation to filings. Nvidia's revenue is a supply-side indicator, but Anthropic's Trainium/TPU/GPU mix demonstrates why a Nvidia-only multiplier cannot precisely measure all AI infrastructure (claims 10, 14, 50).

The required-revenue literature uses different populations and methods. Sequoia's Nvidia-sales multiplier estimates end-user revenue at an assumed gross margin; Bain's 2030 cloud scenario uses sustainable capex/revenue ratios, on-prem migration and reinvested savings. JPMorgan's 10% return estimate is not independently reconstructable from its blog. Goldman's infrastructure break-even and ROIC estimates differ from its application-layer estimate, and the original model was not inspected (claims 11–14). They do not form a chronological series of one statistic. Morgan Stanley estimates a financing gap, not revenue: its named funding categories leave $350bn of the stated $1.5tn gap unspecified (claim 15).

Revenue growth is visible at Google Cloud and in Microsoft's AI run-rate (claims 30, 34). Private lab reporting requires separate treatment: OpenAI's Q2 revenue annualises to $26.8bn, unlike a point-in-time run-rate (claims 33, 59). October reporting puts Anthropic's Q3 revenue at $16.8bn and describes adjusted profitability; this is meaningful counterevidence to a blanket lab-loss characterization, but neither an audited result nor proof of free cash flow (claim 46). Anthropic’s company-stated run-rate baseline is also distinct from full-year recognized revenue (claims 44, 58). Lab receipts overlap suppliers' cloud revenue. Available disclosures do not establish a non-overlapping total of AI end-customer revenue, so they cannot quantify a sector-wide payback gap or establish a revenue-doubling deadline.

Track customer prepayments, lease commitments, supplier credit and new securities alongside gross capex: they determine who funds assets and when losses reach investors. Oracle's cash flow includes $11.363bn of customer advances, and its capex reconciliation reports $17.966bn net cash outlay versus $28.499bn gross (claim 42). CoreWeave's September convertible shows financing remains available, while adding leverage and potential dilution; capped calls and fees reduce deployable proceeds (claim 43). Reuters' Anthropic prospectus reporting describes historical losses, future compute obligations and customer concentration; the reported Broadcom facility adds acceleration and availability risks (claims 44–45). The June Broadcom financing tranche is another channel, with potential overlap across capex and leases (claim 50). These amounts must not be added without eliminating overlap. The unresolved Project Jupiter lead warrants checking contractual payment timing, not assuming a debt default (claim 56).

Scope also changes the interpretation: Amazon's investment includes fulfillment capacity, and internal AI could improve existing advertising, recommendations or software without separate AI billing. Conversely, Google Cloud profit does not isolate new AI-cohort returns, with shared AI R&D partly recorded outside the segment (claim 51). Both sides limit a comparison of total capex with separately billed AI revenue.

### 2. GPU depreciation

Microsoft's quarterly short-lived asset mix is a company disclosure, not an audited sector mix. Server and networking schedules at the other filers are not necessarily GPU-specific lives (claims 5, 17–20). Meta's extension added $2.59bn to 2025 net income, 4.3% of reported annual earnings (claim 54). Accounting schedules affect the timing of earnings, not the physical asset's cash payback.

For the hypothetical fully operating $500bn short-lived pool below, zero-residual straight-line depreciation is $83bn annually over six years, $125bn over four and $167bn over three. The $83bn difference is conditional scenario arithmetic, not an observed big-four misstatement. It does not verify Burry's cumulative or company-specific earnings estimates, which require cohort dates, taxes and earnings denominators (claims 21, 53).

Rental evidence cuts both ways: H100 contract rates rose roughly 40%, and A100 listings remain available (claims 22–23). Listings do not establish achieved utilization or lifetime profitability, and no causal inference about Nebius's extension follows from them. InferenceX's single-model comparison estimates Blackwell's token cost below Hopper's, but the full economic cost assumptions were only partly verified (claim 24). It does not show that every H100 workload survives only under scarcity. Resolution requires realized rental prices, utilization, operating costs and residual values through changing supply conditions.

### 3. Inference unit economics

Some premium tiers and scheduled Google prices rise, while other models become cheaper. OpenAI's Sol and Anthropic's Opus lines counter a universal frontier-price turn (claims 25–27). September's Opus 5.5 input/output cut was 20%, with cheaper cache reads; the vendor's performance assertions are not independent validation (claim 48). Fixed-capability price declines measured by Epoch and MIT can coexist with this mixed list-price pattern (claim 28). Equivalent capability, workload, caching and service tier must be compared before inferring pricing power or scarcity.

The InferenceX cost estimates are workload-specific, not measured commodity-serving margins. Comparing their open-model costs with closed-model API list prices mixes models, input/output composition and service costs (claim 24). OpenAI's reported positive adjusted gross margin does not imply that inference costs included in that measure exceed sales; R&D losses and cash burn can coexist with positive gross margin (claim 32). Anthropic also has a reported historical gross-margin observation and a newer adjusted-profitability report, all C-grade private reporting (claims 46–47). Nvidia is highly profitable; CoreWeave has positive adjusted EBITDA but material losses after depreciation and financing; lab profitability varies and remains incompletely disclosed (claims 49, 55).

Usage is growing, but the series do not establish Jevons. Google's monthly totals span all surfaces, while its API metric is narrower; OpenRouter is a separate reported series (claims 29, 31). Microsoft's trillion-token metric concerns customer counts, not demonstrated growth in each customer's consumption (claim 30). At constant units and mix, sevenfold volume and a 10–40-fold price decline imply revenue falling 30–82.5% (claim 57). Capability-price and aggregate raw-token series are not comparable enough to estimate this effect. Cloud growth is observed revenue growth, not an identification of price elasticity or a causal Jevons effect.

### 4. Training economics

Epoch's final-run cost fit grows 2.4x annually and would exceed $1bn by 2027 if the historical trend continued (claim 36). Its estimates put final released-model runs at 9.6%, 22.6% and 12.3% of R&D compute spending at OpenAI, MiniMax and Z.ai. Filings supply R&D denominators, while modeled training costs supply numerators; the ratios are C-grade estimates, not direct prospectus ratios (claim 37). Experiments and unreleased models also involve training, so these estimates do not make training broadly defined a minority of lab compute. DeepSeek-V3's disclosed final-run cost excludes prior research and ablations (claim 38).

Accounting depends on the contract and asset owner. Owners capitalize hardware and recognize depreciation; power and staff can enter R&D, as illustrated by Meta's expense disclosure (claim 40). Renters, lessees and customers making prepayments can have different recognition patterns; the financing arrangements above rule out a categorical “labs expense, hyperscalers capitalize” rule. OpenAI's research-compute treatment lacks a primary accounting-policy disclosure, and Epoch's two-year amortisation is an assumption (claim 37). API retirement dates do not establish the economic life of model development, research or data, so they cannot establish a one-year model asset life or a GAAP earnings overstatement.

### 5. Scaling laws, efficiency and demand for compute

Epoch's estimated eight-month efficiency halving and MIT's algorithmic contribution concern compute or prices for fixed capability (claims 28, 39). Reasoning and agentic workflows can add computation per task, but Huang's “100 times” compute assertion is not a measured distribution of tokens per answer. Aggregate tokens do not directly measure compute consumption across changing models and hardware. The evidence therefore does not establish that compute demand outgrew efficiency by a particular margin. Cheaper new capacity could erode older assets' pricing power, while greater usage could sustain them; utilization, realized prices and operating costs determine the outcome.

### A worked model

We illustrate capital recovery for an assumed $750bn new cohort, with two-thirds ($500bn) short-lived equipment and one-third ($250bn) long-lived infrastructure; this is not an estimate of AI-only investment. The mix extrapolates Microsoft’s disclosure, and the 15-year long life is assumed, not a sector-wide accounting policy (claim 53). Revenue R at owner EBITDA margin m repays each tranche with discount rate r. Pre-tax, R = [500 / A(r,L) + 250 / A(r,15)] / m, where A(r,n) = (1−(1+r)^−n)/r. Required annual revenue in $bn:

| GPU life L | r 8%, m 50% | r 10%, m 50% | r 12%, m 50% | r 10%, m 30% | r 10%, m 60% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 years | 446 | 468 | 490 | 780 | 390 |
| 4 years | 360 | 381 | 403 | 635 | 318 |
| 5 years | 309 | 330 | 351 | 549 | 275 |
| 6 years | 275 | 295 | 317 | 492 | 246 |

At six-year equipment life, 50% owner EBITDA margin and 10% discount rate, the hypothetical cohort requires $295bn annually; at three years, $468bn. An exactly $1.36tn hypothetical new cohort requires $536–848bn under the same assumptions. That extension does not value the remaining obligations of previously deployed cohorts: it ignores past recovery and remaining lives. Goldman's $417bn can be matched numerically at approximately 39.5% EBITDA margin with five-year life or 35.4% with six years, but that does not confirm its capital base or return definition; ROIC is not automatically an annuity discount rate (claims 14, 53).

Over the selected ranges, equipment life changes required revenue by 1.6x, while a margin change from 50% to 30% changes it by 1.7x. Raising r from 8% to 12% adds 10–15%, depending on life. The displayed margin sensitivity is slightly greater than the life sensitivity; these ranges do not prove a universal ranking (claim 53). A renter's gross margin cannot substitute for an owner's EBITDA margin: rental payments already fund the owner's hardware. Recovering that capex again would mix layers.

The model attributes all assumed investment to incremental revenue and omits replacement assets, conventional cloud, logistics, internal AI benefits, commissioning delays, taxes, working capital, declining utilization/prices and residual values; land is not modeled separately. It requires the short-lived revenue component for L years and the long-lived component for 15 years, not one constant combined stream forever. A company-by-company revenue bridge and commissioning/cash-flow schedule are needed to quantify an actual payback gap or a doubling deadline. Three-year equipment life is mathematically possible at sufficient revenue; five-plus years is not a model requirement.

## Conflicts and open questions

- **Useful life and earnings.** Burry argues for a much shorter economic life than filers' server schedules (claims 17–21). The original note judged rentals supportive of longer lives, H100 viability dependent on scarcity, and its depreciation sensitivity supportive of Burry's earnings estimates. The cross-check agrees life matters but finds none of those conclusions established by listings or one benchmark. Realized utilization, costs, cohort timing and company earnings denominators are needed; the scenario does not resolve the dispute.
- **Largest uncertainty and who bears losses.** The original note ranked GPU life first. The cross-check finds the selected margin sensitivity slightly larger and emphasizes customer solvency, contract terms and funding independently. Its judgment is that highly committed lab and leveraged-provider equity is exposed early, with lenders, lessors and suppliers affected according to protections; cash stress can begin at customer or provider depending on guarantees and prepayments. This is a risk assessment, not an established loss sequence.
- **Pricing and Jevons.** The original note read rising premium list prices as a universal frontier reversal and token growth as proof of Jevons. The cross-check points to Sol and Opus cuts and rejects the inference from unmatched token/capability-price series. The corrected fixed-unit arithmetic runs against the original revenue claim. Pricing power versus temporary scarcity remains unresolved without comparable workloads and paid-volume data (claims 25–30, 48, 57).
- **Revenue gap and deadline.** The original note proposed $150–250bn end revenue, a narrowing gap and doubling by 2028. The cross-check finds no reproducible revenue bridge, matching asset population or timing schedule. Those estimates are withdrawn from the active analysis; run-rates and booked quarters remain distinct and overlap supplier revenue. Published required-revenue scenarios also differ in layer and return definition (claims 11–14, 30, 33–34, 46).
- **Profitability by layer.** The original note described neoclouds as roughly break-even after depreciation and interest and labs broadly as cash-burning. The cross-check finds CoreWeave's material net losses directly contradict that characterization, while Anthropic's reported Q3 adjusted profitability is counterevidence to a blanket lab claim. Positive gross margin does not prove inference costs exceed sales; adjusted profit does not prove durable free cash flow. Anthropic's reported historical losses and newer adjusted result use different periods and metrics, with no primary accounts inspected (claims 32, 44, 46–47, 49).
- **Lab training accounting and model life.** The original note treated MiniMax/Z.ai final-run shares as direct filing disclosures and inferred roughly one-year model value and earnings flattery from endpoint retirements. The cross-check identifies modeled numerators and rejects the economic-life inference. We retain the ratios as C-grade Epoch estimates and leave OpenAI's accounting unresolved pending a primary policy disclosure (claim 37).
- **Training-cost interval within the source.** The cross-check specifies 95%, matching Epoch's abstract for the 2.4x trend. The fetched publication also has a revision note saying intervals were switched to 90%. Claim 36 uses the abstract's 95% label; the source's inconsistent presentation remains unresolved rather than implying the whole publication uses one interval.
- **Primary access and unresolved subclaims.** Goldman and Morgan Stanley remain bank-model summaries; McKinsey's figure was available only through a snippet (claims 14–16). Google's historical 3 Pro price was not independently recovered in the cross-check (claim 27). Access limits do not establish falsity. Project Jupiter's reported force-majeure notice is unverified; Oracle's unchanged-scope statement does not resolve contractual payment timing (claim 56).
