# AI unit economics: can the capex pay back?

*Last updated: 2026-11-05 · Cross-checked by: Claude Fable 5.1, Codex gpt-6-astra · Data: [ai-unit-economics-capex-by-year.csv](/data/ai-unit-economics-capex-by-year.csv), [ai-unit-economics-api-prices.csv](/data/ai-unit-economics-api-prices.csv), [ai-unit-economics-useful-life.csv](/data/ai-unit-economics-useful-life.csv)*

## Summary

- The illustrative $750B new cohort needs $295–468B annual owner revenue at a 50% EBITDA margin and 10% discount rate, depending on equipment life. This establishes a conditional hurdle, not an observed payback gap (claim 3).
- Required-revenue estimates differ in capital cohorts, timing and revenue layer. Gartner’s cumulative owner receipts cannot be compared directly with Patel’s annual lab revenue path (claims 10, 14).
- Profitability varies across the stack. Anthropic’s filed adjusted operating profit coexists with a GAAP operating loss and expensive short-term compute. Cloud growth and segment profit coexist with cash pressure at Alphabet and Amazon; OpenAI’s margin remains reported, unverified (claims 28–29, 32).
- Customer advances, supplier credit and new securities fund assets and allocate risk. Disclosed supplier backstops make that transmission concrete; they do not create end-user revenue or eliminate the underlying compute obligation.
- The largest uncertainty is sustained cohort cash recovery: life, utilization, owner margin and customer funding interact. The selected margin sensitivity exceeds the life sensitivity (claim 5).
- API prices move in both directions. Growing usage alone establishes neither paid-revenue growth nor a causal Jevons effect (claims 18–21). Final released-model runs also capture only part of research compute (claim 23).

## Analysis

The capex totals mix company definitions, fiscal calendars, leases and non-AI assets. The current guidance sum and historical spending therefore cannot identify an AI-only cohort. Delivery shifts can lower calendar-year spending without lowering intended investment. Oracle and CoreWeave add spending on different bases (claims 1–2, 26–27).

The worked model prices annual capital recovery at the infrastructure owner. Its equipment and infrastructure revenue components last for their respective assumed lives. It omits replacement assets, conventional cloud, logistics, internal AI benefits, commissioning delays, taxes, working capital, changing prices/utilization and residual values; land is not separate. A renter’s gross margin already includes rental payments funding the owner’s hardware and cannot substitute for owner EBITDA. The $1.36T extension is another fresh cohort, not a valuation of obligations remaining on already deployed assets (claim 3).

Sequoia’s flow multiplier, Bain’s sustainable spending ratio and Goldman’s return estimates answer different questions. Gartner accumulates receipts over a spending program. IoT models adequate returns now followed by deterioration as capital accumulates. Brookings assumes mature utilization of completed capacity. Patel’s inference revenue density requires paid demand, capability advantage and deployment to persist as capacity expands. Its lab receipts still need an owner-capture bridge. Normalizing capital makes Brookings much closer to the project scenario, though margin definitions differ (claims 6–7, 9–15).

Lab receipts overlap cloud suppliers’ revenue. The [cloud results](#key-claims) show accelerating demand alongside cash pressure, not a measured AI-only return (claim 32). Google Cloud includes TPU systems sales and excludes shared AI research expenses from segment profit. Alphabet’s quarterly and Amazon’s trailing cash deficits cannot be added as same-period observations. Microsoft’s positive cash generation is counterevidence to a universal funding shortfall. The [demand-side note](/research/macro/demand-side) holds end-user scenarios.

Anthropic’s filing supplies a revenue and operating-profit reconciliation (claim 29). Short-term compute costs squeeze adjusted margins even as revenue grows; excluding noncash donation charges does not exclude that compute expense. Annualizing quarterly sales measures pace, not recurring contracted revenue. Its filed payment schedule replaces speculation about aggregate obligations; liquidity, future capital raising and customer collections still determine affordability. The [Anthropic note](/research/stack/anthropic) owns those figures.

Financing changes timing and loss allocation. Amazon’s discretionary secured hardware facility is borrowing capacity, not drawn cash. Broadcom’s disclosed residual-value backstop and Google’s executed TeraWulf support put supplier balance sheets behind customer obligations; guarantees, leases and project principal cannot be added as separate spending. SpaceX’s lease commitment remains conditional on closing. Customer advances improve current cash while adding future obligations. Terms and amounts belong in [circular financing](/research/macro/circular-financing) and the [issuer notes](/report/stack).

Accounting lives determine earnings timing, not physical cash payback. Rental listings establish availability, not achieved utilization or lifetime profit. The single-workload benchmark cannot establish that every older GPU survives only under scarcity. Realized rents, costs and residual values through changing supply conditions would resolve more than accounting schedules alone (claims 4, 17, 30).

Fixed-capability price declines can coexist with premium tiers and scheduled price increases. Comparable workload, caching, tier and paid volume are needed to infer pricing power. Aggregate tokens across changing models and hardware cannot identify compute demand or price elasticity (claims 18–21, 25).

Final released-model runs exclude experiments and unreleased models that also involve training. Their small modeled shares do not make training broadly defined a minority of lab compute. Owners capitalize hardware; rented capacity, leases and prepayments have different recognition patterns. API retirement dates do not establish the economic life of research or models. OpenAI’s research-compute accounting still lacks a primary policy disclosure (claims 22–24).

## Open questions

- What cohort utilization, realized rents, operating costs and residual values support the assumed lives and margins?
- Can Anthropic replace costly short-term compute with committed capacity quickly enough to sustain margins? Its filed reconciliation and obligation schedule improve visibility; durable cash coverage remains the test. OpenAI still lacks equivalent public accounts.
- Can the original Goldman model establish capital vintages and owner capture? Patel’s inference-versus-fleet allocation is also unresolved. SpaceX management claims rapid infrastructure payback, but supplies no cohort cash-flow bridge; see the [labs note](/research/stack/labs-other).
- Epoch’s training-cost abstract and revision note disagree on confidence-interval labeling (claim 22). The historical Gemini Pro price could not be independently recovered.
- Silicon’s dated prints and undated FAQ disagree. The [GPU-pricing note](/research/macro/gpu-pricing-and-utilization) owns monthly coverage checks; pricing alone leaves utilization unresolved (claim 30).
