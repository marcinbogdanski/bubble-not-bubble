# AI unit economics: can the capex pay back?

*Last updated: 2026-10-17 · Status: draft · Author: Claude (Fable 5.1 subagent) · Reviewer: none*

## Summary

- The four largest hyperscalers spent about $231bn on capex in 2024 and $381bn in 2025, and have guided to roughly $730bn for 2026 (Amazon ~$200bn, Microsoft ~$190bn, Alphabet $195–205bn, Meta $130–145bn). Oracle (FY26 $55.7bn, Q1 FY27 alone $28.5bn) and CoreWeave ($35–39bn guided) add roughly another $100bn+. Goldman's "about $800bn" for 2026 hyperscaler capex is consistent with the filings.
- The required-revenue arithmetic has moved from Sequoia's $600bn (2024) to $650bn (JPMorgan, 10% return), $417bn (Goldman, 10% ROIC on infrastructure alone) and $2tn by 2030 (Bain). Our own model: the 2026 cohort alone needs roughly $300–470bn of annual revenue at a 50% EBITDA margin and 10% cost of capital, depending on whether GPUs last six or three years; the 2024–26 cohorts together need $535–850bn.
- Revenue is appearing faster than in 2024–25: Google Cloud grew 82% y/y in Q2 2026 to $24.8bn, Microsoft's AI run-rate was $37bn (April 2026), and Anthropic and OpenAI report run-rates of $65bn and about $70bn (reported, unverified; OpenAI's booked Q2 2026 revenue of $6.7bn annualises to only $27bn). AI-attributable revenue in 2026 is therefore plausibly $150–250bn, against a required $300–470bn for the 2026 cohort alone. The gap is narrowing but is still a gap.
- Depreciation is the fulcrum. Microsoft says two-thirds of its capex is short-lived GPUs/CPUs; the five big filers depreciate servers over 5–6 years (Amazon shortened a subset to 5 in 2025, Meta lengthened to 5.5). On the 2026 cohort, a 3-year rather than 6-year GPU life would raise annual depreciation by about $83bn across the big four. Rental-market evidence cuts both ways: H100 contract rates rose roughly 40% from October 2025 to March 2026 and A100s (launched 2020) still rent at a $1.84/hr median, which supports lives beyond three years; but Blackwell serves tokens at less than half the cost of Hopper, which caps what old chips can earn.
- Inference prices for a fixed capability fall 5–10x a year (MIT) or faster (Epoch: 9x–900x), yet frontier list prices stopped falling in late 2025 and are now rising: GPT-5 was $1.25/$10 per million tokens, GPT-5.5 is $5/$30, GPT-6 Astra $10/$50; Google will double Gemini 4 Argon to $4/$20 on 1 December 2026 and double its Flash tiers on 1 January 2027. Volume growth dwarfs price declines (Google: 9.7tn to 3.2 quadrillion tokens a month in two years), so Jevons is, for now, real.
- Training is a minority of lab compute: final training runs were about 10% of OpenAI's 2024 R&D compute and 12–23% at MiniMax and Z.ai; frontier run costs grow 2.4x a year and will pass $1bn by 2027 (Epoch). Labs expense compute as R&D (OpenAI reportedly amortises research compute over "multiple years"); hyperscalers capitalise the hardware and expense power and staff.
- Single biggest uncertainty: the economic life of a GPU under the 2026 price regime. It decides both the payback arithmetic (a factor of 1.6x in required revenue) and whether reported earnings at Meta, Oracle and Microsoft are overstated by the 20–27% Burry claims.

## Analysis

### 1. The capex payback math

The filings put big-4 capex at $231bn in 2024, $381bn in 2025 and about $730bn guided for 2026 (claims 1–7). The definitions differ (Amazon reports gross purchases, Microsoft adds finance leases, Meta adds lease principal) and the 2026 numbers moved during the year: Microsoft guided ~$190bn in April, of which $25bn is memory and component inflation rather than more capacity, and Alphabet lifted its top end to $205bn in July. Oracle is on a different fiscal year and a different trajectory: capex $55.7bn in FY26 against operating cash flow of $32.0bn, so free cash flow was −$23.7bn, and Q1 FY27 capex of $28.5bn annualises above $110bn (claim 8). Adding Oracle and CoreWeave, the 2026 build is in the $830–900bn range, and Goldman's ~$800bn for hyperscalers alone is in the same place (claim 14). Nvidia's data-centre revenue is the supply-side check: $193.7bn in FY26 and $89bn in the latest quarter (claim 10), i.e. a $356bn run-rate of GPU and networking sales flowing into this capex.

The "required revenue" literature all runs the same arithmetic with different multipliers. Sequoia (2024) took Nvidia's run-rate, doubled it for the rest of the data centre and doubled again for a 50% end-user gross margin, and got $600bn (claim 11); on today's Nvidia run-rate the same 4x gives $1.4tn. Bain ties $500bn of annual capex to $2tn of annual revenue at sustainable cloud capex-to-revenue ratios and finds the world $800bn short by 2030 (claim 12). JPMorgan Asset Management puts $650bn of annual revenue as the price of a 10% return (claim 13). Goldman's September 2026 note, which we have only through summaries, gives ~$300bn to break even, ~$417bn for a 10% ROIC on the infrastructure layer, and ~$1tn of application-layer revenue if application developers are to keep healthy margins too (claim 14). Morgan Stanley's number is a financing gap, not a revenue gap: $2.9tn of data-centre capex over 2025–28, of which hyperscaler cash flow covers ~$1.4tn, leaving ~$1.5tn for private credit, bonds and ABS (claim 15).

Where revenue stands is harder. Google Cloud's growth accelerated to 82% in Q2 2026 and its operating income tripled to $8.8bn; Microsoft's AI run-rate reached $37bn in April and Azure passed $100bn for the fiscal year (claims 30, 34). The labs' numbers are reported and unverified: Anthropic told investors its run-rate passed $65bn in July 2026, and OpenAI's is reported near $70bn, but OpenAI's booked Q2 revenue was $6.7bn, which annualises to $27bn (claim 33). Run-rates annualise the latest month; booked revenue lags; and all of these overlap (Anthropic's revenue is partly Amazon's and Google's cloud revenue, and OpenAI's is partly Microsoft's). A defensible 2026 range for AI-attributable end revenue is $150–250bn. That is above Sequoia's 2024 "$100bn or so" of assumed AI revenue, and roughly at Goldman's break-even line, but below every return-earning threshold.

### 2. GPU depreciation

Microsoft discloses that about two-thirds of its capex is short-lived assets, "primarily GPUs and CPUs", with the rest supporting "monetization over the next 15 years and beyond" (claim 5). Depreciation life on the short-lived two-thirds is therefore the single most important accounting assumption in the sector. The filings say: Alphabet six years (since 2023), Microsoft two to six, Oracle six (lengthened from five in FY25), CoreWeave six (lengthened from five in January 2023), Meta 5.5 (lengthened from five in January 2025), Amazon six, except that from January 2025 a subset of servers and networking equipment went back to five "due to the increased pace of technology development, particularly in the area of artificial intelligence and machine learning" (claims 17–19). The tracker count is telling: 16 of 17 useful-life changes since 2020 lengthened lives; Amazon's 2025 reversal is the exception, and Nebius lengthened again in January 2026 (claim 20).

The earnings sensitivity is large but bounded. Meta's half-year extension added $2.59bn (10% of a quarter's worth) to 2025 net income; Amazon's 2024 extension added $2.5bn and its 2025 partial reversal cost $1.0bn (claims 17–18). On the 2026 cohort alone, $500bn of short-lived assets depreciates at $83bn a year over six years, $125bn over four and $167bn over three: a three-year life would cut big-4 pre-tax earnings by about $83bn a year relative to six. Burry's $176bn over 2026–28 and 21–27% overstatement at Meta and Oracle (claim 21) is in the same order of magnitude as that calculation, so the claim is arithmetically plausible even if his two-to-three-year premise is the contested part.

What does the market say about actual life? Two things, pulling opposite ways. First, old chips still earn money: A100s, launched in 2020, rent at a $1.84/hr median across 47 providers, and H100 one-year contract rates rose about 40% between October 2025 and March 2026 while on-demand medians sit around $3.39/hr (claims 22–23). Scarcity in 2026 is extending economic lives, which is why Nebius and others lengthened. Second, the cost curve is brutal: on SemiAnalysis's benchmarks a GB200 NVL72 serves tokens at $0.19 per million against $0.42 for an H100 at the same interactivity, and the gap widens at higher throughput (claim 24). An H100 can be kept busy at today's rental prices only because demand exceeds Blackwell supply; when supply catches up, an H100's rent must fall towards its cost-per-token disadvantage. A six-year straight line is defensible for an asset that keeps running; it is not defensible for the revenue the asset earns in years four to six, which is what matters for payback.

### 3. Inference unit economics

Three price series are in the data file. The headline is the shape of the curve, not its slope. From GPT-4 in 2023 ($30/$60 per million tokens) to GPT-5 in August 2025 ($1.25/$10), OpenAI's flagship price fell 24x on input. Since then it has risen: GPT-5.2 $1.75/$14, GPT-5.4 $2.50/$15, GPT-5.5 $5/$30, and a new top tier at $10/$50 (GPT-6 Astra), while GPT-6 Sol resets the mainstream flagship to $2/$10 (claim 25). Anthropic's Opus fell from $15/$75 to $4/$20, but its new Fable tier sits at $10/$50 (claim 26). Google is the clearest signal because it has pre-announced increases: Gemini 4 Argon doubles to $4/$20 on 1 December 2026 and the Flash tiers double on 1 January 2027 (claim 27). Per unit of capability prices still fall fast: Epoch measured 9x–900x a year depending on benchmark, and MIT's narrower frontier estimate is 5–10x a year, of which about 3x is algorithmic (claim 28). But the price of the best available model has stopped falling, and the providers are explicitly rationing compute through price.

The cost side explains why. Serving a mid-size open model on owned GB200s costs $0.06–0.19 per million tokens at hyperscaler scale (claim 24), which against $2–10 list prices implies very high gross margins for commodity serving. Frontier closed models are far larger and run with long reasoning traces, and the only lab margin figure we have, reported from OpenAI's internal documents, is an adjusted gross margin of 33% in 2025, down from 40%, with inference costs quadrupling and spot compute bought at short notice (claim 32). CoreWeave, which sells the raw capacity, earned a 5% adjusted operating margin and an adjusted net loss in Q2 2026 on $2.58bn of revenue (claim 9). The margin stack in 2026 is therefore: Nvidia 75% gross; neoclouds roughly break-even after depreciation and interest; labs at 30–40% gross and burning cash; application layer unknown.

Does lower price raise total spend? The volume data say yes, overwhelmingly. Google went from 9.7tn tokens a month in May 2024 to 480tn in May 2025 and over 3.2 quadrillion in May 2026, a 330x increase in two years, and its API alone now processes 22bn tokens a minute (claim 29); OpenRouter's weekly volume doubled between March and May 2026 (claim 31); Microsoft has 300 customers each on track to process a trillion tokens in a year, accelerating 30% quarter on quarter (claim 30). Even at 10–40x annual price declines per unit of capability, a 7x volume increase on a fixed model and far more on cheaper models means revenue grows. Google Cloud's 82% growth is the realised version of this. The open question is whether that revenue is at the lab or at the cloud, and whether it covers the compute at the lab (OpenAI's falling gross margin says not yet).

### 4. Training economics

Epoch's series puts the amortised hardware and energy cost of frontier final runs on a 2.4x-a-year trend, passing $1bn by 2027 (claim 36). But final runs are a minority of lab compute: about 10% of OpenAI's roughly $5bn of 2024 R&D compute, 22.6% at MiniMax and 12.3% at Z.ai per their Hong Kong IPO filings (claim 37). Most "training" spend is experiments and unreleased models, and a replication at the end (DeepSeek-V3's 2.8m H800-hours, $5.6m at rental rates, excluding its research) is cheap relative to the search that produced it (claim 38).

Accounting differs by owner. Hyperscalers capitalise the GPUs (that is the capex above) and run training as depreciation plus power and staff in R&D: Meta's R&D rose 31% to $57.4bn in 2025 "mostly due to higher employee compensation and infrastructure costs" (claim 40). Labs that rent compute expense it, although OpenAI's research compute is reported to be amortised over "multiple years" (Epoch assumed two). A model's economic value depreciates faster than any of these schedules: Anthropic retires models roughly 12–18 months after release and the price of a given capability falls 5–40x a year (claims 26, 28), so a frontier model's revenue-earning life is about a year. Treating a training run as a multi-year asset flatters near-term earnings in the same way a six-year GPU life does.

### 5. Scaling laws, efficiency and demand for compute

The two efficiency findings are consistent: compute for a fixed language-model capability halves every eight months or so (Epoch), and about 3x a year of the frontier price decline is algorithmic (MIT) (claims 28, 39). On its own that is deflationary for compute demand per unit of capability. Against it stands test-time compute: reasoning models spend 10–100x more tokens per answer (Huang's "100 times", claim 39), providers now charge for "thinking" tokens and have effort settings that default to high, and agentic workloads chain many calls. The token volumes in section 3 are the net result: demand for compute grew faster than efficiency improved, by a wide margin, in 2024–26. The risk for the payback arithmetic is not that efficiency gains make demand disappear; it is that they hand demand to whoever has the cheapest capacity, so that a 2024 GPU cohort loses pricing power to the 2026 cohort before it is paid off.

### A worked model

Take the big-4 2026 cohort, $750bn. Two-thirds ($500bn) is short-lived with life L; one-third ($250bn) is buildings, power and land with a 15-year life. Revenue R, earned at EBITDA margin m, must repay each tranche with a return r. Pre-tax, R = [500 / A(r,L) + 250 / A(r,15)] / m, where A is the annuity factor. Required annual revenue in $bn:

| GPU life L | r 8%, m 50% | r 10%, m 50% | r 12%, m 50% | r 10%, m 30% | r 10%, m 60% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 years | 446 | 468 | 490 | 780 | 390 |
| 4 years | 360 | 381 | 403 | 635 | 318 |
| 5 years | 309 | 330 | 351 | 549 | 275 |
| 6 years | 275 | 295 | 317 | 492 | 246 |

At the sector's own assumptions (six-year life, 50% margin, 10% cost of capital) the 2026 cohort needs about $295bn of incremental annual revenue; at a three-year life, $468bn. Extending to the 2024–26 cohorts ($1.36tn), the requirement is $535bn (six years) to $847bn (three years) at 10% and 50%, bracketing JPMorgan's $650bn. Goldman's $417bn for a 10% ROIC is consistent with a 5–6 year life on the 2026 cohort plus a thinner margin.

What matters most, in order: (1) GPU economic life, worth 1.6x on required revenue between six and three years; (2) EBITDA margin on AI revenue, worth 1.7x between 50% and 30% (OpenAI's 33% gross margin, before opex, suggests the lab layer is near the low case); (3) cost of capital, worth only ~10% between 8% and 12%, because the assets are short-lived. For the payback to work, AI revenue must therefore roughly double from the ~$150–250bn of 2026 by 2028 while the margin on it rises, and GPUs must keep earning for five-plus years even as each generation halves the cost per token. The revenue growth half of that is currently happening (cloud growth of 40–80%, token volumes up 7x). The margin and life halves are not yet in evidence.

## Conflicts and open questions

- **Useful life.** Burry says 2–3 years; filers say 5–6, Amazon's partial reversal to five being the only shortening. Rental data support longer lives (A100s still rent, H100 contract rates rose 40%), but the per-token cost gap between Hopper and Blackwell says old chips keep earning only while capacity is scarce. Resolution requires rental-price series through a period of Blackwell oversupply, which has not yet occurred. Nvidia's reported memo to analysts rebutting Burry was not retrievable and is not cited.
- **List prices up, unit prices down.** Epoch and MIT measure price per capability (falling fast); the provider pages show flagship prices rising since late 2025, with Google pre-announcing doublings. Both are true; the question is whether rising flagship prices reflect pricing power (good for payback) or compute scarcity passed through (temporary).
- **What counts as AI revenue.** Run-rates (Anthropic $65bn, OpenAI ~$70bn) are reported, unaudited and overlap with cloud revenue; OpenAI's booked quarter annualises to $27bn. The required-revenue estimates also disagree on scope (Goldman: infrastructure vs application layer; Bain: all cloud). No source gives an audited total.
- **Goldman, Morgan Stanley, McKinsey.** All three are cited through summaries because the primary documents timed out or are not public; figures are graded C accordingly.
- **Margins at the lab layer.** One leaked figure (OpenAI 33%) is the entire evidence base. Anthropic's gross margin is not public. The S-1s expected from both would resolve this.
- **Lab training accounting.** The reported multi-year amortisation of OpenAI research compute is from secondary reporting; the Z.ai and MiniMax prospectuses are the only primary disclosures of final-run share.
