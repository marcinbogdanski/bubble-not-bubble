# Historical precedents for the AI investment boom

*Last updated: 2026-10-17 · Cross-checked by: Claude claude-fable-5-1, Codex gpt-6-astra · Data: [historical-precedents-drawdowns.csv](/data/historical-precedents-drawdowns.csv), [historical-precedents-ipo-counts.csv](/data/historical-precedents-ipo-counts.csv)*

## Summary

- Useful railways, electrification, telecom and the commercial internet coexisted with large investor losses. These selected episodes do not establish a failure frequency, common absorption period or universal recovery order. Telecom and dot-com overlap.
- Financing structure shapes how losses spread. Leverage, collateral, maturity mismatch and guarantees can transmit losses beyond equity holders. Equity-led booms can also coincide with banking stress (claims 7, 9, 19–20).
- Telecom vendor finance is a useful mechanism comparison. Lucent disclosed $5.391B undrawn and $1.263B drawn customer credit; Cisco’s annual inventory allowance expense was $2.775B. Nvidia’s conditional $105B guarantee and Amazon’s $50B investment have different loss mechanics (claims 7–8, 23–24).
- Historical warning signals have mixed timing. Lucent’s cuts followed the equity peak; mortgage and funding-market stress preceded the housing-era equity peak. Annual IPO observations cannot establish quarterly leads (claims 16, 18, 22).
- Broad credit spreads, sector repricing, tightening and earnings-supported equities give mixed current signals. Issuance alone does not prove a capex cash deficit, and the evidence does not identify a reliable bubble-cycle stage (claims 25–27).
- The central uncertainty is whether demand and margins can cover financing, operation and replacement costs. Hardware and durable infrastructure have different lives; cheap acquisition alone does not ensure profitable reuse. The Brookings comparison is a scenario, not committed or realised spending (claim 28).

## Analysis

Railway Mania, electrification, telecom, dot-com equities, Japan, housing and crypto/SPACs illustrate mechanisms rather than independent statistical trials. Railways, electricity, telecom and the commercial internet became useful despite large investment losses. Japan and housing had no comparable technology thesis; crypto’s payments thesis is not demonstrated by price recovery. Selecting busts supplies no denominator of booms that avoided a crash.

Railway financing relied heavily on partly paid retail shares and subsequent capital calls. Odlyzko estimates losses at about a third of invested capital (claim 1). The concurrent banking crisis prevents treating this as a clean example of equity finance without financial-system stress (claim 19). Utilities added margin and holding-company leverage. Japan linked bank credit to land collateral; mortgage securitisation, repo and off-balance-sheet vehicles connected housing to funding markets. Crypto-linked uninsured deposits provide another transmission channel (claim 20).

The recovery observations measure nominal prices, excluding dividends and inflation. Index membership can change. They do not restore failed firms’ equity or measure physical utilisation. Railway recovery is unmeasured, and sector-wide telecom recovery is not established. These differences prevent a causal ranking of recovery times by financing structure.

Telecom is the closest vendor-finance comparison. Lucent’s customer credit exposed the supplier to buyer funding, while Cisco’s allowance expense illustrates inventory risk (claims 7–8). WorldCom’s bankruptcy followed the telecom equity peak (claims 6, 9). Continued network operation did not guarantee returns to original investors or profitable reuse of every asset. Capex contracted sharply (claim 30), but technology and regulation also mattered. Spare fiber can be rational when the alternative is reopening trenches (claim 21); low utilisation alone cannot identify irrational investment.

Warning timing depends on the instrument and reference peak. Lucent’s guidance and rating cuts confirmed deterioration after equities turned (claim 22). Mortgage and money-market stress preceded the housing-era equity peak even though the broad rating spread gave a less alarming reading (claims 5, 18). Annual IPO counts and first-day returns document market conditions, not quarterly closure dates (claim 16). SPAC redemptions motivate watching contract reductions while new deals continue, without equating backlog revisions with cash redemptions.

For AI, equipment makers invite scrutiny of cancellations, inventories and receivables. Incumbent carriers are a possible foundry comparison, but regulatory protection complicates the analogy. Utilities and established railways suggest examining hyperscalers’ committed projects. CLECs and wholesalers offer a neocloud stress scenario involving debt, customer concentration and renegotiation; their collapse is not an evidenced base case. WorldCom motivates counterparty analysis without implying equivalent fraud or proving that a customer connecting several vendors is historically unprecedented.

Nvidia’s contingent guarantee, Amazon’s equity investment and [AMD’s purchase-linked warrant](/research/stack/amd-broadcom-custom-silicon) require separate exposure analysis (claims 23–24). Loans, warrants, guarantees and capacity backstops cannot be summed as financed GPU revenue. External finance supplements operating cash, but issuance alone cannot establish a contemporaneous cash deficit. Current sector repricing and tightening coexist with earnings-supported equity gains (claims 25–27).

The Brookings scenario separates hardware from buildings and power, and cautions against assuming housing-style systemic risk (claim 28). No universal GPU economic life is established here. Adequate returns require demand, margins and financing terms to cover operating and replacement costs; ownership transfer and a lower purchase price are insufficient by themselves.

## Open questions

- Aggregate valuation in the utilities-era crash remains disputed by [Bierman](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/the-1929-stock-market-crash/) and [McGrattan–Prescott](https://www.minneapolisfed.org/research/staff-reports/the-1929-stock-market-irving-fisher-was-right). Sector-loss endpoints do not identify which sector turned first.
- Odlyzko’s railway-loss estimate remains uncertain; Campbell and Turner’s differing assessment of investor sophistication has not been verified here. Concurrent financial stress does not establish railway-loan causation.
- Speculative overbuild, regulatory uncertainty and rational spare-fiber installation remain competing telecom explanations. Comparable utilisation, acquisition-cost and operating-return series would help distinguish them; no verified absorption interval is available. The employment source also conflicts with its own endpoints (claim 29).
- Contract-level guarantee exposure, customer concentration and profitable reuse remain unresolved. Quarterly IPO leads, a historical vendor-counterparty census and systematic current guidance cuts are not established.
