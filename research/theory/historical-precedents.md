# Historical precedents for the AI investment boom

*Last updated: 2026-10-17 · Status: draft · Author: Claude (claude-fable-5-1 subagent) · Reviewer: none*

## Summary

- Seven episodes are compared for mechanism, not resemblance: the British Railway Mania (1840s), US electrification and the 1929 utilities/RCA boom, the 1997–2002 telecom and fiber build-out, the dot-com equity bubble, Japan's 1980s credit bubble, the 2008 US housing bubble, and the 2021–22 crypto/SPAC episode.
- Three regularities hold across every episode. (1) The technology thesis was right in six of seven (crypto is the exception), yet investors in the build phase lost most or all of their capital. (2) The overbuilt capacity was used, but by new owners who bought it out of bankruptcy or far below cost, and only after 3–10 years. (3) Drawdowns in the leading index ran 55–90% and took 2–34 years to recover in nominal terms (see `data/historical-precedents-drawdowns.csv`).
- Financing determines the damage. Equity-funded booms (railways, dot-com, SPACs) produced deep equity losses but no banking crisis. Debt- and credit-funded booms (1929 holding companies and margin, telecom vendor financing and bonds, Japan, 2008) produced bank and vendor losses and longer recoveries.
- The closest single analog to today's circular deals is telecom vendor financing: Lucent had $5.4 bn of undrawn and $1.3 bn of drawn customer credit commitments at September 2000, Nortel $4.1 bn unfunded at December 2000, and Cisco charged $2.8 bn to its inventory allowance in FY2001. Today Nvidia carries guarantees capped at $105 bn on OpenAI's leases, AMD issued OpenAI a warrant on 160 m shares at $0.01, and Amazon invested $28.7 bn in OpenAI's Series C while its customers' demand is the reason for Amazon's capex.
- Leading indicators that turned before or at the top: IPO counts and first-day returns (fell from 476 and 71% in 1999 to 80 and 14% in 2001), the sector index diverging from the broad index (Nasdaq telecom index −62% in 2000 while incumbents fell <15%), vendor guidance and rating cuts (Lucent, December 2000), and policy tightening (BOJ 2.5% to 6.0% between May 1989 and August 1990). Credit spreads lagged in 1929, 2000 and 2007 and are not a timing tool.
- Current readings (October 2026): hyperscaler capex is being financed partly with new equity and debt (Alphabet raised $49.6 bn of equity in June 2026; Meta issued $24.9 bn of notes in May 2026); Baa–Aaa spread 0.44 pp (September 2026), the tightest reading in the window examined; US IPO count 90 in 2025 against a 1999 peak of 476. By the historical checklist the boom is in the financing-escalation stage, not yet the indicator-reversal stage.
- Biggest uncertainty: whether AI compute depreciates like fiber (long-lived, re-sold cheaply, absorbed over a decade) or like 1990s PCs (obsolete in 3–5 years). That single parameter changes who keeps the infrastructure and whether second owners can run it profitably.

## Analysis

### The seven episodes

**British Railway Mania (1845–50).** Displacement: steam railways, already profitable on short lines (the Liverpool & Manchester paid 10% dividends), plus a Parliament that sanctioned 4,538 miles in 1846 alone (claim 5). Financing: almost entirely equity from individuals, through partly paid shares on which companies made "calls" as construction progressed; no bank channel of note. Scale: cumulative capital rose from £88.5 m to £245.8 m between 1845 and 1850, and the 1847 peak year alone was 7.3% of GDP (claims 1–2). Outcome: revenue reached £15 m in 1852 against an implied expectation of £60 m; shares of the strongest line fell 55% and weaker lines 80%+; Odlyzko estimates a third of the capital was lost (claims 3–6). Construction continued through 1850 because sunk capital made completion better than abandonment, which is why the index bottomed in 1849–50 rather than at the 1845 peak. Thesis: right. Mileage went from 2,441 (1845) to 10,433 (1860) and railway revenue was 6% of GDP by 1905 (claim 7). The capacity was used, but by the companies as consolidated under later management and at returns far below those promised.

**US electrification and 1929.** Displacement: electrification and radio. RCA rose from 85 to 549 in a year (claim 9); utilities were 18% of NYSE value and traded at price/earnings of 28–39 on the Wigmore sample (claims 10–11). Financing: this was the first credit-layered boom. Utility holding companies pyramided debt and preferred stock on operating assets (holding-company price/book 4.44); investment trusts sold ~$1 bn of new paper in eight months of 1929; retail buyers used 10% margin (claims 8, 11). The first crack was sector-specific and regulatory: the Massachusetts utilities commission refused Edison Electric of Boston a stock split on 11 October 1929 and told it to cut prices; utilities then fell 55% between end-September and end-November against 48% for industrials (claim 10). Drawdown: Dow −89% to July 1932, recovered November 1954 (claim 8). Credit spreads did not lead: Baa–Aaa was 1.32 pp in September 1929 and only reached 5.59 pp in mid-1932 (claim 12). Thesis: right; electricity and radio diffused throughout the 1930s. Who lost: holding-company equity, investment-trust holders (Goldman Sachs Trading Corporation went from $104 a share to under $3 by 1933, Bierman) and margin accounts; the Public Utility Holding Company Act of 1935 then dismantled the pyramids. Conflict: McGrattan and Prescott (Minneapolis Fed Staff Report 294) estimate that, counting intangible capital, stocks were *under*valued at the 1929 peak; Bierman also argues "obviously overpriced" is a myth except for utilities. The 1929 lesson is therefore less about aggregate valuation than about leverage layered on one sector.

**Telecom and fiber, 1997–2002.** This is the closest mechanism match and gets the most space. Displacement: the Telecommunications Act of 1996 plus dense-wavelength multiplexing, which raised capacity per fiber strand from 2.5 Gbps (1996) to 100 Gbps (2000) (Richmond Fed). Demand narrative: "Internet traffic doubles every 100 days", attributed to WorldCom and repeated by BellSouth's CEO in 1999; measured growth was roughly doubling per year (claim 22). Capex: public telecom service providers went from $47 bn (1995) to $121 bn (2000) and back to $49 bn (2002) (claim 13). Financing had three layers: (i) equity for CLECs, whose market cap went from $3 bn (1996) to $86.4 bn (1999) and then $3.77 bn (Feb 2002) (claim 14); (ii) bonds, with WorldCom alone carrying $41 bn of debt at filing (claim 21); (iii) vendor financing by the equipment makers, which is the analog to today's circular deals. Lucent's FY2000 10-K shows $5.4 bn of credit commitments not yet drawn and $1.3 bn drawn, plus $1.4 bn of debt guarantees; it had also started selling customer loans into an off-balance-sheet trust ($579 m sold by September 2000) (claim 17). Nortel disclosed up to $4.1 bn of unfunded customer financing at December 2000, explicitly to "start-up customers" and 3G operators, and warned it would have to hold the loans longer because third-party lenders were retreating (claim 19). Cisco's inventory allowance charge went from $339 m in FY2000 to $2,775 m in FY2001 (claim 20). The sequence of turning indicators was: Nasdaq telecom index peak 10 March 2000 (same day as the Composite); CLEC equity collapse through 2000; vendor guidance cut and rating downgrade (Lucent, 21 December 2000); "fiber glut" reporting early 2001; Nortel's $19 bn quarterly loss in June 2001 (Richmond Fed); WorldCom bankruptcy July 2002 with $103.8 bn of assets. Drawdown: the telecom index fell 84% from peak to May 2003; employment −22% (claims 15–16). Thesis: right; traffic did keep doubling yearly and the fiber was eventually lit. Who kept the infrastructure: creditors and acquirers of bankrupt carriers. WorldCom's equity went from ~$150 bn to under $150 m while its network kept running under new ownership (claim 21). The absorption period is the one number this note could not pin from a primary source; the Richmond Fed paper describes "massive overcapacity" in long-haul fiber in 2001–03 without a utilisation figure, and the often-quoted "under 5% of fiber lit" is not verified here (see Conflicts).

**Dot-com equities, 1995–2002.** Displacement: the commercial internet. Financing: equity, through the IPO window; 476 IPOs in 1999 with a 71% average first-day return and 380 in 2000, versus 80 in 2001 (claim 25). Valuation: CAPE 44.2 in December 1999, the highest in the series back to 1871 (claim 24). Drawdown: Nasdaq −77.9% over 31 months, nominal recovery after 15.1 years (claim 23). Leading indicators: IPO first-day returns and counts collapsed within two quarters of the peak; Baa–Aaa barely moved (0.69 pp in March 2000, 0.81 in December 2000) and only widened to 1.41 pp by October 2002, so credit lagged equity by two years (FRED). Thesis: right. Who lost: equity holders, mostly retail and venture; no systemic banking loss, which is why the 2001 recession was mild.

**Japan, 1986–90.** Displacement: financial deregulation, the post-Plaza yen and a discount rate held at 2.5% for 27 months (claim 29). Financing: bank credit against land, with corporate and household funding growing ~14% a year in 1989 and bank capital inflated by unrealised equity gains (BOJ). Scale: capital gains on stocks and land 452% of GDP over 1986–89 (claim 28). Turning indicator: policy. The BOJ raised the discount rate from 2.5% to 6.0% in five steps between May 1989 and August 1990; equities peaked one step in, land fifteen months later (claims 27, 29). Drawdown: Nikkei −81.9%, with the trough 19 years after the peak and nominal recovery after 34 years; land −80% by 1999 (claims 26–27). Who lost: banks, then taxpayers (¥60 tn of public funds, 12% of GDP) (claim 30). Thesis: there was no technology thesis; this is the pure-credit control case.

**US housing, 2003–09.** Displacement: securitisation and the originate-to-distribute model. Financing: mortgage debt, repo and off-balance-sheet vehicles. Turning indicators: the first large subprime lender (New Century) failed in April 2007, six months before the S&P 500 peak and ten months after the house-price peak (claims 31–33). Spreads lagged again: Baa–Aaa was 0.82 pp at the October 2007 equity peak (claim 34). Drawdown: house prices −27% nominal, trough 67 months after the peak; S&P 500 −57%; recovery of the house-price index after 10.5 years (claims 31–32). Who lost: households, banks, then taxpayers. Thesis: none.

**Crypto and SPACs, 2020–22.** Displacement: zero rates plus a retail trading channel. Financing: equity and tokens, not bank credit. SPAC IPOs went from 59 (2019) to 613 (2021) to 86 (2022), and the redemption rate on completed deals rose from under 10% in Q1 2021 to 60% in Q4 2021, i.e. the cash was being withdrawn while the listing count was still high (claim 36). Operating-company IPOs went from 311 (2021) to 38 (2022) (claim 25). Bitcoin fell 76.7% in twelve months and regained its high in 2.3 years; FTX filed on 11 November 2022, after the bottom was nearly in (claims 35, 37). Who lost: retail equity and token holders, some venture funds (FTX raised $1.8 bn from equity investors). No banking loss of note. Thesis: for crypto as a payments technology, not demonstrated; the asset recovered on sentiment and ETF demand rather than on usage.

Three patterns run through the table. First, the technology being right did not protect capital deployed in the build phase in any episode. Second, drawdowns were deeper and recoveries longer where credit, not equity, carried the boom: 2.3–15 years for equity-funded episodes, 10–34 years for credit-funded ones. Third, the overbuilt asset was always used eventually, but ownership changed through bankruptcy or distressed sale first, and the second owner's cost basis is what made the economics work.

### What the precedents imply for AI in 2026

**Which precedent fits which layer.**

- *Chip equipment (ASML, Applied, Lam, KLA):* 1840s railway contractors and ironmasters, and 2000-era optical component makers. They sell picks and shovels on orders that can be cancelled; the historical pattern is a sharp but short earnings drop and no balance-sheet damage. Watch order-book cancellations, not valuation.
- *Foundry (TSMC):* the incumbent telephone carriers of 2000. BellSouth and Qwest fell less than 15% in 2000 while CLECs fell 63% (claim 14); the monopoly asset with diversified customers was the safest place in the stack. Risk is utilisation of leading-edge capacity, not solvency.
- *GPUs (Nvidia, AMD):* Lucent, Nortel and Cisco in 2000. The match is now explicit: Nvidia has guarantees capped at $105 bn on OpenAI's data-centre leases (claim 42); AMD has issued OpenAI a warrant on 160 m shares at $0.01 that vests on purchases (claim 43). Lucent's undrawn commitments were 1.0x its drawn balance before the loss cycle began; the 2026 analogs are an order of magnitude larger relative to the vendors' revenue and are concentrated on one counterparty. The 2001 precedent is a sudden inventory and receivables charge (Cisco $2.8 bn, claim 20) within 12 months of the customer financing peak.
- *Hyperscalers (Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta):* 1920s utilities and the 1840s established railways. They are profitable, their capex is an order of magnitude larger than the telecom peak (Microsoft $115.9 bn, Amazon $169 bn trailing, Meta $130–145 bn guided, claims 38–41), and, like the 1840s lines, they will keep building once committed because completion beats abandonment. The new 2026 fact is that they are no longer funding capex purely from cash flow: Alphabet raised $49.6 bn of equity and Meta $24.9 bn of notes (claims 39, 41). In 1929 terms, that is the point when utilities moved from retained earnings to holding-company paper.
- *Neoclouds (CoreWeave, Nebius, Lambda):* the CLECs and long-haul wholesalers (Global Crossing, Level 3). Single-product, customer-concentrated, debt-financed at 7–15% with delayed-draw facilities (claim 45) and dependent on vendor relationships. The CLEC equity path (−63% then −88%, claim 14) is the base case if contracts are renegotiated; the assets would survive under new owners.
- *Labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI):* WorldCom plus the dot-coms. WorldCom was the demand narrative's author and the largest credit; its $41 bn of debt sat on top of the whole fiber chain (claim 21). OpenAI is now the counterparty behind Nvidia's guarantees, AMD's warrant, Amazon's $28.7 bn Series C investment and Oracle's RPO jump from $138 bn to $638 bn (claims 40, 42–44). In 2000, no single customer tied together three vendors' balance sheets; in 2026 one does.

**Which indicators to watch now, from history.**

1. *Vendor commitments as a share of vendor revenue and their drawn fraction.* Lucent's shift from committed to drawn and the move to off-balance-sheet loan trusts came within two quarters of the top. Track Nvidia's guarantee effectiveness dates (first phase FY2029), AMD warrant vesting, and any sale or securitisation of customer receivables.
2. *IPO window.* 1999–2001 and 2021–22 both show the window closing within two to three quarters of the equity peak. 2025 had 90 IPOs with a 29% first-day return, well below 1999 but rising (claim 25). A lab or neocloud IPO with a >50% first-day pop would match the 1999 pattern.
3. *Sector index vs broad index.* The telecom index fell 62% in 2000 while incumbents fell under 15%. A split between neocloud/lab-exposed equities and the hyperscalers would be the first sign.
4. *Guidance and ratings on the vendors, not the buyers.* Lucent cut guidance and was downgraded in December 2000, nine months after the index peak and before the fiber-glut reporting. Equipment and GPU vendors see cancellations first.
5. *Policy tightening.* Japan's bubble ended only after five rate rises. The current cycle has no equivalent tightening yet; this indicator is quiet.
6. *Credit spreads are confirmation, not warning.* Baa–Aaa was 1.32 pp in September 1929, 0.69 pp in March 2000 and 0.82 pp in October 2007. It is 0.44 pp in September 2026 and HY OAS 3.23 pp (claim 47). Spread widening will date the bust, not predict it.
7. *Redemption-style exits.* 2021 SPAC redemptions rose from under 10% to 60% while new listings continued (claim 36). The AI analog is customers reducing committed take-or-pay volumes while headline deal announcements continue; Oracle's RPO is the series to watch for downward revision.

## Conflicts and open questions

- **Was 1929 overvalued?** Bierman (EH.net) and McGrattan–Prescott argue no for the aggregate market; Bierman says yes for utilities. This note follows the sector reading: aggregate valuation metrics were not the signal, sector leverage was.
- **Railway loss size.** Odlyzko's "about a third" of £250 m is his own estimate pending a fuller study; Campbell and Turner's work on the same episode, which argues investors were less naive than Odlyzko implies, could not be fetched within this task's search budget.
- **Fiber utilisation after 2002.** The widely repeated figure that only a few percent of long-haul fiber was lit in 2001–02 was not found in a primary or central-bank source here and is not used. The Richmond Fed paper confirms "massive overcapacity" qualitatively. The time to absorb the glut is therefore stated as 3–10 years without a hard end-date; this is the most important unverified number for the AI analogy because it bounds how long second owners waited.
- **Nortel's $19 bn quarterly loss** is from the Richmond Fed paper citing press reports, not Nortel's own filing (grade B).
- **Global Crossing** figures (assets, debt, sale price) were not fetched; the text refers to it only as a long-haul wholesaler named by both Fed papers.
- **Insider selling** in 2000 and 2021 was requested in the brief; no primary dataset was fetched and no claim is made.
- **Depreciation of AI compute.** None of the precedents involved an asset that both costs tens of billions per site and may be technologically obsolete in 3–5 years. If GPUs depreciate like PCs, the fiber lesson (second owners run it profitably at a low cost basis) does not transfer and losses fall on first owners in full. This is the open question the stack notes must answer.
- **Recovery times** are nominal price indices without dividends or inflation; total-return recoveries are shorter (the Nasdaq total return recovered in 2014 by one secondary account), and real recoveries are longer.
- **Current hyperscaler financing** is from 10-K/10-Q filings fetched in this task; the Meta–Blue Owl Hyperion joint venture reported in late 2025 was not found in Meta's Q2 2026 10-Q text search and is not cited.
