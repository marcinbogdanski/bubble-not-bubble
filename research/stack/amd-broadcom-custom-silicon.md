# The non-Nvidia accelerator layer: AMD, Broadcom, Marvell, hyperscaler silicon, Intel, Cerebras/Groq

*Last updated: 2026-10-17 · Status: draft · Author: Claude (claude-fable-5-1 subagent) · Reviewer: none*

## Summary

- The non-Nvidia accelerator layer has gone from marginal to very large in twelve months. Broadcom's AI semiconductor revenue was $16.7bn in the quarter to 2 August 2026 (+221% y/y), it guides $21.7bn for the next quarter and management says it has "secured the supply" to reach ~$115bn in FY2027 and ~$230bn in FY2028. AMD's Data Center segment did $6.7bn in Q2 2026 (+107% y/y) and guides company revenue to ~$13bn in Q3. These are audited (Broadcom, AMD) or 8-K-filed numbers, not projections.
- The demand is concentrated in a handful of buyers, several of which are private and loss-making. Broadcom has six XPU customers; Anthropic is "on track to become our largest XPU customer in 2027", OpenAI the second-largest. AMD's two headline deals (OpenAI, Meta) are 6 GW each. The layer's growth is therefore a leveraged bet on the labs' ability to pay, not on broad enterprise demand.
- The financing is circular in a way that Nvidia's own deals are not: AMD pays its two biggest customers in equity. Each of OpenAI and Meta holds a warrant for 160m AMD shares at $0.01 that vests on gigawatt milestones and AMD share-price targets up to $600. At $600 that is ~$96bn per customer; SemiAnalysis models the warrants as taking OpenAI's effective GPU capital cost towards zero if the share price reaches the hurdles. AMD's 10-Q classifies the warrants as liabilities and says none had vested at 27 June 2026.
- Custom silicon does lower capex per unit of compute. SemiAnalysis estimates Google's all-in TCO per Ironwood chip ~44% below a GB200 server and 30–41% below for external renters; JPMorgan prices Ironwood at ~$13k vs $35–40k for B200/B300; Amazon claims Trainium2 is 30–40% better price-performance and Trainium3 a further ~40%; Microsoft claims Maia 200 is 30% better performance per dollar than its fleet. But hyperscaler capex is not falling ($630–690bn for the big four in 2026): cheaper compute is being bought in larger quantities, so the aggregate revenue hurdle is unchanged. What changes is who captures the margin (Broadcom and the hyperscalers instead of Nvidia).
- Profitability is uneven. Broadcom is the most comfortably profitable company in the whole AI stack on cash terms (free cash flow $13.7bn, 46% of revenue, in one quarter), though it carries ~$59bn of debt and its gross margin is falling (78% → ~73% guided) as low-margin XPU and pass-through memory grow. AMD is profitable but cash-light (Q2 FCF margin 14%) and is now taking on $30bn of purchase commitments, ~$14bn of data-centre leases, a $5bn Anthropic investment and up to ~20% potential dilution. Marvell is GAAP-thin. Intel is state- and Nvidia-backed with no disclosed AI accelerator revenue. Cerebras is public, profitable on GAAP in 2025 only because of one-offs, and 86% dependent on two UAE customers.
- The single biggest uncertainty is whether the lab contracts that underpin Broadcom's $179bn remaining performance obligations and AMD's 12 GW of warrant-linked commitments are honoured on schedule. Both companies' outlooks assume OpenAI and Anthropic deploy ~15 GW of custom silicon and GPUs between them in 2027, which requires the labs to raise capital on a scale that has not yet been demonstrated.

## Analysis

### What this layer is

Nvidia's merchant GPUs have four substitutes: AMD's merchant Instinct GPUs; custom accelerators (XPUs) designed by the buyer and built by Broadcom (Google TPU, Meta MTIA, OpenAI "Jalapeño") or Marvell (Amazon Trainium, Microsoft Maia, historically); the hyperscalers' own programmes that sit on top of those vendors; and independents (Intel Gaudi, Cerebras, Groq). Broadcom also sells the Ethernet switching that connects both custom and merchant clusters, which is ~27% of its AI revenue and carries a higher margin than the XPUs (claims 17, 21).

### AMD: real revenue, bought with equity and balance-sheet commitments

AMD's Data Center segment went from $3.2bn in Q2 2025 (with an $800m MI308 write-down and an operating loss) to $6.7bn and $2.1bn of operating income in Q2 2026 (claims 1, 4). The segment mixes EPYC CPUs and Instinct GPUs and AMD does not break out GPU revenue, so the "AI" share is not observable from filings. Management guides company revenue to ~$13bn in Q3 and says data center revenue should more than double in 2027 (claim 2; SiliconANGLE).

The step-change in the order book came from three deals: OpenAI (6 GW, October 2025), Meta (6 GW, up to $60bn, February 2026) and Anthropic (2 GW, July 2026), plus Oracle (50,000 MI450) and Microsoft (Helios on Azure). What makes the first two unusual is the payment mechanism. AMD issued each of OpenAI and Meta a warrant for 160m shares at one cent, vesting in tranches tied to gigawatts delivered *and* to AMD's share price reaching targets up to $600 (claims 6, 7). The 8-K is explicit that the first OpenAI tranche vests on "delivery of the initial one (1) gigawatt", so AMD has structured the warrant as a volume rebate paid in stock.

Three consequences follow. First, the economics to the customer are a function of AMD's share price: at the $600 final hurdle each warrant is worth ~$96bn, which SemiAnalysis models as reducing OpenAI's GPU capital cost per GPU-hour from ~$2.50 to ~$1.75 at August 2026 prices and to roughly zero at $600–700 (claim 14, grade C). The customer's incentive is therefore to buy from AMD *and* to see AMD's stock rise, which is the definition of a reflexive arrangement. Second, the cost to AMD is borne by existing shareholders through ~19–20% dilution if both warrants vest, not through the income statement, which is why the CFO can describe the deal as "highly accretive" to non-GAAP EPS (claim 5). Third, as of the Q2 2026 10-Q no shares had vested and the warrants are liability-classified pending equity-classification conditions (claim 8), so the first tranche's vesting (on the first OpenAI gigawatt, scheduled from 2H 2026) is the first hard milestone that can be observed in a filing.

AMD's balance sheet is also being committed in a way it was not a year ago: $30.3bn of unconditional purchase commitments ($17.4bn due in the rest of 2026), $14bn of data-centre leases either unstarted or signed after quarter-end, up to $4.1bn of lease guarantees for "commercial partner" data centres, and up to $5bn of equity for Anthropic (claims 9, 10). Free cash flow was $1.6bn in the quarter on $11.5bn of revenue (claim 3). AMD is thus moving from a fabless vendor to a company that pre-buys wafers and memory, guarantees partners' leases, and leases capacity itself, while paying its two largest customers in equity. The forward targets (>60% data-center CAGR, ~$100bn in five years, claim 13) are only achievable if the labs' gigawatts materialise on schedule.

### Broadcom: the most profitable business in the stack, with a concentrated and lengthening order book

Broadcom's AI semiconductor revenue has compounded from $3.7bn (Q4 FY24, derived) to $16.7bn (Q3 FY26), and the company guides $21.7bn for Q4, ~$58bn for FY26, ~$115bn for FY27 and ~$230bn for FY28 (claims 14–16). Unlike AMD's warrants, Broadcom's model is conventional: it builds what the customer designs, is paid in cash, and has visibility through firm contracts. Remaining performance obligations were $179.2bn at 2 August 2026, of which only ~25% is due within twelve months (claim 18), which means roughly $135bn of contracted revenue extends beyond a year, lengthening sharply from the "$73bn over 18 months" of December 2025 (claim 19).

The strength is cash: $13.7bn of free cash flow in one quarter, 46% of revenue, against $59bn of debt that is a legacy of VMware, not of the AI build (claims 14, 22). Two things are weakening at the margin. Gross margin is guided from 78% a year ago to ~73%, because XPUs carry lower margins than networking and because rack-scale systems pass memory through at cost (claim 21). And concentration is extreme: six XPU customers, with Anthropic set to be the largest in 2027 and OpenAI the second (claim 17). Both are private, loss-making labs whose ability to pay for 5 GW (Anthropic, FY27) plus 1.3 GW (OpenAI, 2027) of Broadcom silicon, on top of their Nvidia, AMD and cloud commitments, depends on continued capital raising. Broadcom's counterparty risk is partly intermediated (Google Cloud rents TPUs to Anthropic and Meta; Google has reportedly offered Anthropic up to $40bn), but the Q2 call made clear that Broadcom also sells ~$10bn of TPU racks directly to Anthropic (claims 20, 25, 29).

Note the slippage on OpenAI: the October 2025 release said rack deployments "starting in the second half of 2026"; by December Hock Tan said "we don't expect much in 2026" and described 2027–2029; by September 2026 the commitment is 1.3 GW "in 2027" (claims 17, 19, 23). The direction of the programme is intact; the timing has moved right by roughly two quarters.

### Hyperscaler silicon: the capex-per-compute question

The evidence that custom silicon is cheaper per unit of compute is consistent across sources but mostly estimated. SemiAnalysis puts Google's internal TCO per Ironwood chip ~44% below a GB200 server and the external rental price 30–41% below GB200/GB300; JPMorgan prices Ironwood at ~$13k against $35–40k for Blackwell; Amazon claims 30–40% better price-performance for Trainium2 and a further ~40% for Trainium3; Microsoft claims 30% better performance per dollar for Maia 200 (claims 26, 27, 30, 34). The mechanism is simple: Nvidia's ~75% gross margin is replaced by Broadcom's lower silicon margin plus the hyperscaler's own design cost.

The inference for the bubble question is two-sided. Per unit of compute, custom silicon reduces the revenue needed to earn a return by roughly a third. But the hyperscalers are not reducing capex: Amazon guides ~$200bn for 2026, Alphabet $175–185bn, and the big four together at least $630bn (claims 29, 30). They are buying more compute at a lower unit price. The aggregate revenue hurdle on the installed base is therefore unchanged; what custom silicon changes is the distribution of margin (less to Nvidia, more retained by Google/Amazon/Microsoft and paid to Broadcom/Marvell) and the resale value of the assets (a TPU has no secondary market outside Google, which matters if demand disappoints).

Google is the clearest case of a hyperscaler turning its chip into an external product: Anthropic (up to 1m TPUs, >1 GW in 2026, "tens of billions"), Meta (rental deal reported, purchase talks for 2027), a reported leasing JV, and Bloomberg's report that DeepMind researchers queue behind external customers (claims 24, 28, 29). The Google Cloud blog, however, only offers Ironwood through Google Cloud; outright external sales run through Broadcom. Amazon's Trainium is at >1.4m Trainium2 chips with Trainium3 supply "nearly all committed" by mid-2026, but Anthropic is the overwhelming customer and Amazon's up-to-$33bn equity in Anthropic against Anthropic's $100bn AWS commitment is the same vendor-finances-customer loop seen elsewhere (claims 30, 31). Microsoft's Maia 200 is the least mature: deployed in two regions, customer access pending, and Microsoft simultaneously signed up as AMD's Helios lighthouse customer (claim 34).

### Marvell, Intel, Cerebras, Groq

Marvell's data center revenue grew 46% y/y to $2.2bn with management promising "significant acceleration" in custom silicon from 2H FY27 (claim 32), but it is GAAP-thin ($308m net income on $2.7bn), has reportedly lost the next Trainium generation to Alchip, and the "new Tier 1 XPU program" that underpins the FY28 doubling is unnamed (claim 33). It is the most exposed of the public names to a single customer decision.

Intel is not a competitive accelerator vendor today: Gaudi revenue is undisclosed, Crescent Island is only sampling in 2H 2026, and the company's 2025 recapitalisation came from the US government ($8.9bn for ~9.9%, funded by converting grants Intel had already been awarded) and from Nvidia ($5bn, in exchange for Intel building custom x86 CPUs for Nvidia's platforms) (claims 35–37). The Nvidia deal strengthens Nvidia's system, not Intel's accelerators.

Cerebras is the one independent with public accounts. Revenue grew 76% to $510m in 2025, but 86% came from two Abu Dhabi entities, the GAAP profit coincided with a non-GAAP loss, and its ">$20bn" OpenAI contract came with a warrant for 33.4m shares at effectively zero and a $1bn loan *from* OpenAI to Cerebras (claim 38). The IPO priced at $185 and the stock closed day one near $311 (claim 41, grade C). Groq's exit was a ~$20bn licence-and-hire by Nvidia, which removed the most credible independent inference challenger from the market while avoiding a merger review (claim 39).

### Who is comfortably profitable, who is betting

Comfortably profitable: Broadcom (cash-generative, contracted, diversified across six buyers and networking). Profitable but newly leveraged to its customers' success: AMD (thin FCF, large commitments, equity-funded rebates). Thin: Marvell. Loss-making or dependent on outside capital: Intel, Cerebras (customer-concentrated), the labs themselves. The hyperscaler programmes are cost centres inside companies that fund them from operating cash flow; the risk sits in the parents' capex, covered in their own notes.

## Conflicts and open questions

- **Share shift: estimates disagree by an order of magnitude.** Morgan Stanley (Dec 2024) had ASICs at 13% of accelerator *sales* in 2027; JPMorgan (Jun 2026) has ASICs at 53% of *units* in 2027; SemiAnalysis has Nvidia >90% of the market in Q4 2025; New Street has Nvidia at 20–30% of *inference* by 2028 (claims 27, 40). Units and dollars diverge because an Ironwood is priced at roughly a third of a Blackwell. On Broadcom's own guidance ($115bn FY27) plus AMD's (data center more than doubling) the non-Nvidia dollar share rises materially in 2027, but a dollar figure for Nvidia's 2027 data center revenue is outside this note; the Nvidia note should be read alongside.
- **Does custom silicon reduce the required revenue, or just move margin?** The TCO estimates (claims 26–27) are from one analyst house and one bank, not from filings. Google and Amazon have never disclosed TPU or Trainium unit costs. The counter-evidence is Broadcom's falling gross margin and rising "content per gigawatt", which shows the custom route is not costless, and the hyperscalers' rising capex, which shows savings are being reinvested rather than banked.
- **Timing slippage.** OpenAI–Broadcom moved from "2H 2026" to "1.3 GW in 2027"; the first OpenAI gigawatt from AMD is "2H 2026" but no shipment has been confirmed in a filing; Anthropic's first AMD gigawatt is 1H 2027 (claims 10, 17, 23). The AMD warrants vest on delivery, so the Q3 and Q4 2026 10-Qs will show whether the schedule held.
- **Who pays if the labs cannot?** Broadcom's $179bn RPO and AMD's 12 GW are contracts with OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta. Only Meta has the cash flow to fund its commitment internally. If the labs' funding tightens, Broadcom has supply it has already "secured" (prepaid wafers and HBM, implied by its inventory doubling to $4.5bn) and AMD has $30bn of purchase commitments. Neither company discloses cancellation terms.
- **Unverified claims.** The Meta–Google TPU rental's value and term; Google's "up to $40bn" for Anthropic; Marvell's loss of Trainium to Alchip; the Groq price; Cerebras's $24.6bn RPO; Intel's reported miss of a $500m Gaudi target in 2024 (not re-verified from a fetched page); whether Nvidia's Intel investment has closed. The Tom's Hardware May 2026 survey of custom ASICs and the Broadcom Q3 call page on Nasdaq could not be retrieved (paywall/timeout).
- **What would resolve it.** AMD's Q3 2026 10-Q (warrant tranche vesting, Data Center revenue); Broadcom's Q4 FY26 release (whether AI revenue hits $21.7bn and RPO keeps growing); Alphabet and Amazon disclosures of external TPU/Trainium revenue; any lab financing round that references chip commitments.
