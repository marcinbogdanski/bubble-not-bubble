# Microsoft

*Last updated: 2026-10-17 · Status: draft · Author: Claude (claude-fable-5-1 subagent) · Reviewer: none*

## Summary

- Microsoft is the largest hyperscaler by AI-related backlog and OpenAI's main commercial partner. In fiscal 2026 (ended 30 June 2026) it spent $145.3 billion on capital expenditure including finance leases, up 65% on FY2025's $88.2 billion, against operating cash flow of $182.9 billion. Cash capex (the cash-flow-statement line) was $115.9 billion, so free cash flow was $67.0 billion, down from $71.6 billion a year earlier while net income rose 31% to $133.7 billion.
- The build is still funded from operations, but the margin of safety has narrowed: capex consumed 63% of operating cash flow (38% in FY2024), free cash flow after dividends and buybacks was about $18 billion, cash and short-term investments fell $18 billion to $76.8 billion, and the balance sheet picked up $66.6 billion of finance-lease liabilities (from $46.2 billion). The biggest number is off the balance sheet: $329.1 billion of signed datacenter leases that had not yet commenced at 30 June 2026, up from $92.7 billion a year earlier.
- Demand evidence is strong and primary: Azure revenue passed $100 billion in FY2026 (up 41%), Azure grew 43% in the June quarter with 45% guided for September, Microsoft 365 Copilot passed 30 million paid seats, and the "AI business" run-rate was $37 billion in March 2026 (up 123%). Management has said demand exceeded supply in every quarter since April 2024.
- OpenAI concentration is the central risk. Roughly 45% of the $625 billion commercial RPO at December 2025 was OpenAI (about $281 billion, derived); by June 2026 RPO was $678 billion and the OpenAI share is about 41% (derived). FY2026 revenue from OpenAI, including revenue share, was $24.1 billion (7.3% of total) with $6.0 billion receivable. Microsoft owns about 25% of OpenAI (as-converted, 30 June 2026), funded $11.9 billion of a $13.0 billion commitment, and booked a $10.0 billion non-cash dilution gain in the December 2025 quarter after absorbing $4.1 billion of equity-method losses the quarter before.
- The October 2025 restructuring and April 2026 amendment traded exclusivity for volume: Microsoft gave up its right of first refusal on OpenAI compute and its IP license became non-exclusive, in exchange for a $250 billion incremental Azure commitment, IP rights through 2032 and a revenue share through 2030 subject to a cap. Microsoft is now one of several OpenAI compute suppliers rather than the sole one.
- Microsoft has also bought capacity from neoclouds: Nebius ($17.4-19.4 billion, five years), IREN ($9.7 billion, five years), Nscale (about 200,000 GB300 GPUs, value undisclosed) and Lambda (undisclosed), and it was 67% of CoreWeave's 2025 revenue. These commitments do not appear as a line in Microsoft's filings.
- An accounting change from FY2027 (datacenter and office useful life 15 to 25 years) cuts reported calendar-2026 capex from about $190 billion to about $175 billion by moving future datacenter leases from finance to operating leases, with "minimal" earnings benefit. Reported capex is now a less complete measure of the build than it was.
- Biggest uncertainty: whether OpenAI can pay the roughly $280 billion it has committed to Azure over the next several years, and what Microsoft's own lease and neocloud commitments are worth if it cannot.

## Analysis

### What Microsoft is in the stack

Microsoft sells AI three ways: raw Azure capacity to model builders (OpenAI above all, now also Anthropic), Azure AI services to enterprises, and AI features inside its own software (Microsoft 365 Copilot, GitHub Copilot). It also owns about a quarter of OpenAI and collects a revenue share from it. This makes it simultaneously the best-placed hyperscaler on demand evidence and the most concentrated on a single private counterparty.

### Profitability and cash generation

The operating business is in excellent shape. FY2026 revenue grew 18% to $331.8 billion, operating income 21% to $155.2 billion, and operating cash flow 34% to $182.9 billion (claims 1-2). Azure crossed $100 billion of revenue, growing 41% for the year and accelerating to 43% in the June quarter with 45% guided for September (claim 14). Microsoft 365 Copilot doubled its paid seats in six months to over 30 million (claim 17). Management's "AI business" run-rate, which bundles Azure AI consumption by model builders with Copilot products, was $37 billion in March 2026, nearly triple the $13 billion of December 2024 (claim 16). Microsoft stopped disclosing how many points of Azure growth come from AI after March 2025, when it was 16 of 33; the acceleration since then is consistent with AI being most of the increment, but that is inference.

The cost of the build shows up in three places. Microsoft Cloud gross margin fell to 66% for the year and 65% in the June quarter, which the 10-K attributes to AI infrastructure investment (claim 18). Depreciation expense rose 56% to $34.3 billion (claim 9), and finance-lease interest expense rose to $2.5 billion. And cash conversion weakened: free cash flow was $67.0 billion on $133.7 billion of net income, 50% conversion versus 84% in FY2024 (claim 4). Note that GAAP net income includes a $5.0 billion after-tax non-cash gain on OpenAI and a $3.2 billion gain on Anthropic; adjusting for OpenAI, non-GAAP net income was $128.8 billion.

### Is the build funded by cash flow?

Yes, with declining headroom. Operating cash flow of $182.9 billion covered cash capex of $115.9 billion, finance-lease principal of $3.1 billion, dividends of $26.4 billion and buybacks of $22.3 billion with about $15 billion to spare; cash and short-term investments nonetheless fell from $94.6 billion to $76.8 billion because Microsoft also bought $58 billion of investments (claims 2, 3, 12, 13). Debt is small ($40 billion) and was not increased. Capex as a share of operating cash flow went from 38% in FY2024 to 47% in FY2025 to 63% in FY2026.

Management's accrual-basis capex measure, which includes finance leases, was $145.3 billion in FY2026, $30 billion above the cash figure (claim 5). On that measure the quarterly run-rate reached $41 billion in June 2026 and is guided above $50 billion for September (claim 7). If FY2027 capex grows at even half the FY2026 rate, it would be roughly $190-200 billion against operating cash flow that, if it grows with guided double-digit operating income, might reach $200-210 billion. Management says only that it expects to "remain free cash flow positive" (claim 39), which is a markedly weaker statement than a company with $67 billion of free cash flow would normally make. The arithmetic says free cash flow after shareholder returns goes to roughly zero in FY2027 on the current trajectory, and that any further acceleration would need debt. Microsoft can borrow at will, so this is not a solvency question; it is a question of whether the equity story, which has been "AI growth funded internally", survives the transition to a financed build.

### The off-balance-sheet build

The most important single number in the 10-K is $329.1 billion of leases, "primarily for datacenters", signed but not yet commenced at 30 June 2026, commencing between FY2027 and FY2033 with terms up to 20 years (claim 11). That figure was $92.7 billion at June 2025 and $196.6 billion at March 2026: it has grown by $236 billion in twelve months and by $132 billion in the final quarter alone. None of it is on the balance sheet, none is in capex, and the 10-K notes that "some arrangements [are] subject to certain contractual conditions being met". Together with $66.6 billion of recognised finance-lease liabilities ($89.7 billion undiscounted over a 13-year weighted term), Microsoft has committed to roughly $400 billion of datacenter lease payments, most of them to third-party developers who are themselves financing construction against Microsoft's credit. Those leases are the mechanism by which Microsoft's demand forecast becomes other people's debt.

The useful-life change compounds the opacity. Extending datacenter buildings from 15 to 25 years means more leases fail the finance-lease test and become operating leases, which "are included in capital expenditures while operating leases are not" (claim 8). Management's calendar-2026 capex expectation therefore fell from about $190 billion to about $175 billion with "expectations unchanged" outside the accounting effect. From FY2027, reported capex understates the build relative to prior quarters, and the gap will show up instead as operating lease cost (already $7.0 billion in FY2026) and in the uncommenced-lease footnote. The earnings benefit is "minimal" per the CFO, which is plausible because buildings are the minority of the spend: two-thirds of capex is "short-lived assets, primarily CPUs and GPUs" (claim 6), depreciated over up to six years, and that policy is unchanged.

### OpenAI: the concentration

Three OpenAI numbers matter. First, backlog. The CFO said in January 2026 that approximately 45% of the $625 billion commercial RPO was OpenAI, about $281 billion (claim 20). The December quarter's RPO jump of $233 billion against the $250 billion incremental Azure contract implies OpenAI's pre-existing RPO was around $50 billion. By June 2026 all sequential RPO growth came from non-frontier customers, so OpenAI's balance is still roughly $281 billion, about 41% of $678 billion (claim 21). RPO excluding OpenAI grew 25%, in line with Microsoft's historical rate: the 84% headline is almost entirely one customer.

Second, revenue. The FY2026 10-K discloses, for the first time as a related party, $24.1 billion of revenue "from commercial arrangements with OpenAI, inclusive of revenue-sharing payments", 7.3% of total revenue, with $6.0 billion receivable at year end (claim 22). That is the current cash relationship; the $281 billion of backlog is what it is contracted to become. The 10-K does not say how the $24.1 billion splits between Azure consumption and revenue share.

Third, the investment. Microsoft has funded $11.9 billion of a $13.0 billion commitment and holds about 25% on an as-converted basis, down from 27% in March as OpenAI raised more capital (claim 23). The accounting has swung. Under the equity method using HLBV, Microsoft absorbed $1.5 billion of OpenAI losses in FY2024, $4.8 billion in FY2025 and $4.1 billion in the September 2025 quarter alone (claim 24), which the CFO had said were "capped by the amount of investment we make in total" (claim 25). By September 2025 cumulative recognised losses were close to the funded amount. The October 2025 recapitalisation then produced a $10.0 billion dilution gain in the December quarter, which raised the carrying value and in doing so re-opened Microsoft's capacity to recognise its share of future OpenAI losses: the quarters since have been a $19 million loss and a roughly $0.6 billion gain. This is a derived observation, not a company statement, but it means the "other income" line is again exposed to OpenAI's operating losses, which OpenAI's other funders have reported to be large.

The commercial terms moved against exclusivity in two steps. In October 2025 Microsoft gave up its right of first refusal on OpenAI compute and accepted that non-API products can run on any cloud, in exchange for the $250 billion commitment, IP rights through 2032 and a revenue share that continues until an expert panel verifies AGI but with "payments spread over a longer period" (claim 26). In April 2026 the IP license became non-exclusive, OpenAI gained the right to serve all products on any cloud (shipping first on Azure unless Microsoft "cannot and chooses not to" support them), Microsoft stopped paying OpenAI a revenue share, and OpenAI's payments to Microsoft were fixed through 2030 "subject to a total cap" whose size is undisclosed (claim 27). The reported rate is about 20% (claim 28, unverified). Each step converted a strategic hold into a commercial contract. That is better for OpenAI's flexibility and probably for Microsoft's regulatory position, but the exposure Microsoft retains is now mostly a receivable: whether OpenAI pays for $281 billion of Azure.

### Exposure if OpenAI cannot pay

If OpenAI defaulted or renegotiated, the first-order hit would be to backlog, not to reported revenue: $281 billion would disappear from RPO, leaving about $397 billion growing 25%. Current-year revenue at risk is $24.1 billion (7.3%) plus the $6.0 billion receivable, plus whatever share of the September-2025-onwards Azure growth is OpenAI consumption. The second-order hit is the capacity: Microsoft has contracted leases and neocloud capacity sized for OpenAI's commitments, and the 10-K's own risk factor spells out the consequence, "underutilization of infrastructure and ... impairment of assets" (claim 36). The $329.1 billion of uncommenced leases cannot be assumed to be cancellable. Management's answer, in January 2026, was that the fleet is "fungible" and that Anthropic ($30 billion commitment, claim 34) and enterprise demand would absorb capacity; the CFO also said many GPUs "are already contracted for most of their useful life", which is reassuring only if the counterparties pay. The equity stake, carried at perhaps $20 billion after the dilution gain, would be written down, and the "other income" line would absorb losses up to that carrying value.

Microsoft is better placed than Oracle or CoreWeave on this risk: OpenAI is 7% of its revenue, not most of it, and it has the balance sheet to carry idle capacity for years. But it has the largest absolute OpenAI receivable of any company, and the OpenAI contract is the reason its backlog looks like $678 billion rather than $400 billion.

### The neocloud layer

Microsoft has also bought capacity it does not own. Nebius will supply a five-year, $17.4 billion (up to $19.4 billion) tranche programme from Vineland, New Jersey, with Microsoft holding the right to terminate tranches delivered late (claim 29). IREN will supply four tranches at Childress, Texas, for about $9.7 billion over five years; the first was accepted in August 2026 and IREN has borrowed $3.6 billion against the remaining milestones with a parent guarantee that bites if Microsoft terminates (claim 30). Nscale is delivering about 200,000 GB300 GPUs across Texas, Norway, the UK and Portugal with a Microsoft option on a further 700MW in Texas, value undisclosed but reported near $23 billion (claims 31-32). Lambda's deal is undisclosed. Microsoft was 67% of CoreWeave's 2025 revenue (claim 33). Reported total: over $60 billion (claim 32, grade C). These are purchase commitments; Microsoft's 10-K has no contractual-obligations table and they do not appear in its filings. They matter because they are the part of Microsoft's capacity that is financed by the smallest balance sheets in the chain, with Microsoft's credit as the collateral.

### Capacity constraints versus overcapacity

The record is unambiguous on what management has said: every call since April 2024 has reported demand ahead of supply, and the horizon of the constraint has kept moving out: "will remain in H1" (July 2024), "through the first half of our fiscal year" (July 2025), "through at least the end of our fiscal year" (October 2025), "at least through 2026" (April 2026), "continues to exceed available capacity" (July 2026) (claim 35). The CFO's framing in January 2026 is the most useful: Azure guidance is "an allocated capacity guide", and had the GPUs that came online in the first half gone entirely to Azure, "the KPI would have been over 40". In other words, reported Azure growth is supply-limited and management chooses how much capacity to allocate to third parties versus first-party products and OpenAI. That supports the demand story but also means Azure growth is partly a management decision and will not signal softening demand until supply catches up.

The counter-evidence is structural rather than quoted. Capex is growing faster than revenue by a wide margin (65% versus 18%); the backlog growth that justifies it is 25% outside one customer; the uncommenced-lease book tripled in a year; and the accounting changes (longer building lives, leases migrating off capex) are the kind a company makes when the capital intensity has become the story. None of this proves overbuild. It does mean the "capacity constrained" narrative rests on OpenAI and Anthropic continuing to pay for the capacity they have booked.

### What the stock discounts

At $529 Microsoft trades at 29.5 times trailing and 26.8 times forward earnings with a $3.93 trillion market capitalisation (claim 37). On free cash flow the multiple is about 59 times. The market is paying for earnings, which are growing at 20%-plus with margins near 47%, and treating the capex as an investment that will earn Azure-like returns. The two episodes in the year show what moves it: the January 2026 disclosure that 45% of backlog was OpenAI took the shares down about 6% after hours, and the July 2026 quarter, with 43% Azure growth and a capex number that was lower than feared because of the accounting change, took them up 15.5% (claim 38). The stock therefore discounts continued Azure acceleration, a capex path near $175-200 billion, and OpenAI paying. It does not discount a scenario in which free cash flow goes to zero while depreciation keeps compounding at 50% a year, or one in which the OpenAI backlog is renegotiated.

## Conflicts and open questions

- **Capex guidance basis.** Several outlets report "$175 billion for fiscal 2027". The transcript says calendar 2026 ("our calendar year 2026 CapEx investment expectations remain unchanged ... the shift from finance to operating leases adjusts our expectation to approximately $175 billion") and separately that FY2027 capex "will grow year-over-year" from $145.3 billion. The calendar reading is consistent with Q3 FY26 $31.9 billion, Q4 $41.0 billion and two quarters above $50 billion. We use the calendar reading.
- **Capex definitions.** Management's "capital expenditures including finance leases" is accrual-based and does not equal cash PP&E plus stated finance leases (Q1 FY26: $34.9 billion versus $19.4 billion + $11.1 billion). The 10-K's "finance lease ROU assets obtained" ($24.6 billion FY26) also differs from the sum of quarterly stated finance leases ($28.1 billion). The CSV records all three as reported and does not reconcile them.
- **OpenAI share of RPO at June 2026.** Microsoft gave 45% at December 2025 and only "+25% excluding OpenAI" at June 2026. Our ~41% is derived and assumes no change in the OpenAI balance; the Register's $281 billion is also a calculation. Microsoft has not confirmed either.
- **Revenue share rate and cap.** The ~20% rate is reported (Bloomberg via secondary coverage) and the April 2026 "total cap" is undisclosed. The 10-K confirms revenue-share payments exist and that FY26 OpenAI revenue including them was $24.1 billion, but not the split.
- **Equity method and the loss cap.** Our statement that the dilution gain re-opens loss recognition is an inference from equity-method mechanics and the CFO's October 2024 remark; Microsoft has not described its OpenAI carrying value or loss capacity. The Q3 FY26 10-Q's -$19 million and the derived Q4 +$0.6 billion are consistent with either small HLBV losses offset by further dilution gains ("other funding activity") or near-zero recognised losses.
- **Neocloud totals.** Only Nebius ($17.4-19.4 billion) and IREN ($9.7 billion) are verified in SEC filings. Nscale's ~$23 billion, Lambda's amount and the ">$60 billion" aggregate are secondary/tertiary. Microsoft discloses none of these commitments.
- **"$440 billion wipeout".** Social-media and some press coverage described a $440 billion market-cap fall after the January 2026 results; we could only verify a 6% after-hours move from a primary-adjacent source and record the larger figure as unverified (grade D).
- **Open questions.** How much of FY2026 Azure growth was OpenAI consumption? What are the cancellation terms of the $329.1 billion uncommenced leases? What is the revenue-share cap? What is the OpenAI carrying value? Microsoft's Q1 FY27 10-Q (due after 28 October 2026) will update RPO, uncommenced leases, OpenAI related-party revenue and other income, and should resolve the first signal below.
