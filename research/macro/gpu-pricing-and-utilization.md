# GPU pricing, utilization and residual value

*Last updated: 2026-10-25 · Cross-checked by: Codex gpt-6-astra, Claude Opus 5.5 · Data: [gpu-rental-prices.csv](/data/gpu-rental-prices.csv), [gpu-index-daily.csv](/data/gpu-index-daily.csv), [forecast-observations.csv](/data/forecast-observations.csv)*

## Summary

- GPU rents are segmented by generation and contract. OCPI H100 is $3.02/GPU-hour and B200 $9.57; Silicon H100 is $2.88. These readings establish neither aggregate shortage nor glut (claims 1–4).
- B200's October partial-month mean is $8.1338, up 12.3% against September. H100's is $2.8921, up 2.2%. Neither measures utilization or complete-month pricing (claims 6–7).
- CoreWeave's ~$40M/MW-year short contracts show selected cluster premiums. Its 59% adjusted EBITDA margin coexists with a −2% GAAP operating margin and $640M net interest expense. Lifetime capital returns remain unproven (claims 15–16).
- Supplier backstops can protect receipts without measuring independent end-user demand. Paid GPU-hours and comparable renewals remain the main missing denominators (claims 14, 17–18).
- H100 term recovery is a survey result; the composite stayed near $2.80. A vendor appraisal reports used H100 SXM sales at $14,250 and projects low residual values, without an independently verified sale ledger (claims 12–13, 19–20).
- P1 is substantially an aging test at its long horizon. Read it with P1b and fresh-publication coverage; no qualifying complete-month sequence is demonstrated (claims 28–33).

## Analysis

The evidence supports segmented pricing and selected cluster premiums. It does not measure a market-wide supply balance. Silicon standardizes term, geography, platform and variant, then weights providers through a proprietary model. OCPI uses volume-weighted active on-demand transactions and winsorization, excluding reserved contracts; contributor identities and the winsorization parameter are private. FastGPU and Price of Compute record offers. These instruments cannot be spliced into a single rent series.

Newer-GPU firming survives the qualifications, especially B200's monthly comparison (claim 7). H200's unusually high level needs matched configurations, volumes and executable quotes (claim 8). Historical provider-list ratios support segmentation but do not measure realized discounts. Spot interruptibility, node connectivity and contract terms change the product being bought. Posted prices do not establish available inventory.

Contracted occupancy, billed occupancy, uptime and productive utilization differ. Take-or-pay can make idle chips earn revenue; customer resale discounts reveal spare hours without giving fleet occupancy (claim 14). CoreWeave's earnings leave little operating room for faster depreciation, but neither adjusted EBITDA nor a GAAP loss establishes lifetime capital impairment (claim 16). Cohort revenue, replacement spending, financing cash flows and residual recoveries are needed. Prepayments fund construction while creating delivery obligations. Supplier-backed capacity transfers some revenue risk without proving independent final demand (claims 17–18).

Equity absorbs disappointing renewals first. Secured lenders depend on contracted cash flows and recoverable hardware; unsecured and vendor lenders face losses according to priority. Customers risk interrupted service and lost prepayments. Company-wide accounting lives do not validate individual GPU economic lives; the [unit-economics note](/research/theory/ai-unit-economics) owns that comparison.

Term survey recovery supports continued earning potential, but the flat composite limits the claim's breadth (claims 12–13). Used asks, realized sales and modeled valuations remain different evidence. The opposing resale reports need matched configurations and sale ledgers (claims 19–23). Silicon's rental-based residual model cannot independently corroborate rental-driven hardware appreciation.

Efficiency gains increase effective supply and renewal risk. Benchmark ownership costs cannot be substituted for customer rental tariffs: the apparent generation advantage narrows or reverses with the rental basis (claims 25–27). Neither calculation is a replicated service-price comparison.

A fiber-style glut would combine persistent price declines with immediately available connected clusters, failed renewals, distressed sales and unpaid debt despite rising consumption. Shipments precede deductions for warehouses, retirements and unavailable power. Cheap isolated hours and falling token prices alone are insufficient.

P1 is substantially an aging test at its long horizon (claim 32). P1 Yes with P1b No favors aging; both Yes strengthens glut evidence. Apply the declared coverage and fallback rules rather than silently substituting another series (claims 29–31). Retrospective histories and a capture script establish neither fresh trading nor a qualifying complete-month sequence. Use published corrections at resolution and preserve methodology and quality flags.

## Open questions

Silicon's dated card takes precedence over its undated FAQ, but Saturday chart dating needs reconciliation with the business-day publication calendar. OCPI contributor-level volumes and configurations are needed to assess H200 representativeness. Price of Compute's conflicting H100 ranges remain unresolved.

Comparable renewal prices, billed GPU-hours and fleet capacity are missing. So are complete used-sale ledgers and exact GPU counts and effective prices for highlighted bilateral contracts. SemiAnalysis's “Sold Out” term mapping and benchmark precision conflict need clarification.

The overlap between NVIDIA's capacity backstop and cloud commitments remains unknown. Fresh-publication flags, revision records and sufficient overlap observations are needed before forecast scoring. None of the available series supplies an independently verified market-wide utilization denominator.
