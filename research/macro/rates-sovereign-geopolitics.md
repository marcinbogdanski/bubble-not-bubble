## Analysis

### Rates, inflation and financing

The Fed held through summer and raised in September. CPI and PPI measure different prices; neither is the Fed's PCE inflation objective. BEA's September 30 release reports August PCE inflation of 3.4% headline and 3.0% core y/y, with monthly increases of 0.3% and 0.2%; real PCE rose 0.6% m/m. The release incorporates annual revisions beginning January 2021. The minutes’ staff estimates preceded that release: August headline/core PCE was estimated at 3.8%/3.4% on the old basis, or 3.6%/3.2% under the new methodology. BEA’s September 30 print supersedes both; 3.8% must not propagate as the actual August reading. September core CPI of 2.6% is below August core PCE of 3.0%, the reverse of the usual ordering; the reference months and index construction differ. The CPI reading alone does not establish benign underlying inflation. Persistent inflation coexists with consumption growth, rather than demonstrated aggregate contraction from the energy shock. [S1], [S2], [S4], [S5], [S50], [S51]

The minutes released October 7 describe supportive financial conditions and broadly available credit, while identifying AI investment as an inflation and financing-demand driver. Most participants expected another hike by year-end; several judged policy not restrictive or only mildly restrictive. The manager also reported that “yield spreads on hyperscaler debt used to finance AI infrastructure remained wide”. Market commentary cited competition for capital from “heavy private debt issuance to finance the development of artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructure”; the staff reported that “changes in real rates contributed to most of the net increase in longer-maturity Treasury yields”. Some participants warned the buildout “could cause aggregate demand to outpace aggregate supply over the medium term”. These are separately attributed assessments (A), not measured causal attribution. Together they describe late-cycle feedback: AI demand can raise the inflation and financing hurdles the buildout then faces. Supportive aggregate conditions can coexist with wide AI borrowing spreads. [S50]

The ten-year’s October 5 close of 5.31% was the highest since 5.32% on May 14, 2002, above the 2007 peak of 5.26% (comparison B); the real ten-year was 2.95% that day. The real yield is the relevant observed starting point for discounting real cash flows, alongside risk premiums and expected growth; it is not a measured AI-equity discount rate. The minutes’ real-rate attribution concerns their intermeeting period, not the later October sell-off. [S55], [S56]; claim 52

CME's browser-rendered October distribution favors a hold. Futures-implied probabilities change with prices; a hike is not the October base case in that snapshot. This does not answer the separate year-end policy question in the September minutes. [S6], [S50]

Duration links higher discount rates to lower present values, especially where distributable cash lies far ahead. This is an inference, not a measured AI-index duration. SOFR reprices floating debt; Treasury yields and spreads affect new fixed debt. CoreWeave has fixed as well as floating debt. A 25 bp increase adds $2.5m annual interest per $1bn of unhedged floating principal at full repricing (claim 41, B). Overnight SOFR is not the contractual Term SOFR fixing for DDTL 5.5. [S9], [S19]

DDTL 5.0 also requires ≥95% swap coverage after specified deadlines; future requirements do not establish current hedge coverage. DDTL 4.0 limits recourse except for carve-outs, whereas DDTL 5.0 is parent-guaranteed. These distinctions change loss allocation; aggregate October net exposure remains unverified. [S19]

Profitable incumbents withstand funding shocks better than projects requiring repeated raises. Microsoft's and Nvidia's net income does not measure cash remaining after capex or isolate incremental AI returns. Overextension combines construction delays, fixed customer pricing, floating debt and equipment obsolescence. Equity loses first; creditor, landlord and vendor losses depend on collateral, guarantees and recourse. [S19], [S43], [S47]

The September directive rolls Treasury maturities over, reinvests agency principal into bills and permits reserve-management purchases. The New York Fed distinguishes those purchases from stimulus: reserve availability can improve independently of the policy-rate stance. Several September participants judged that stance not restrictive or only mildly restrictive. [S3], [S48], [S50]

[Historical precedents](../theory/historical-precedents.md) illustrate mechanisms rather than timing rules. Japan's asset prices initially rose after BOJ tightening. The technology boom coexisted with Fed restraint: productive technology does not protect leveraged buyers. The local note's “quiet” current tightening indicator conflicts with September's hike. [S20], [S21], [S2]

October 14 is the plausible macro catalyst within that week: headline CPI was 3.7% versus 3.6% consensus and core rose 0.3% versus 0.2%. M1 fell 6.2% (CRWV −15.5%, ORCL −8.8%) against NDX −2.0%, while the two-year rose 3 bp and HY OAS widened 9 bp. Yet the ten-year fell 2 bp and the real ten-year 4 bp. Reuters/LSEG’s roughly 33% October hike probability on October 14 fell to a separately timed CME 15.5% snapshot on October 17; this is not a like-for-like weekly endpoint comparison. [S4], [S6], [S55], [S56], [S58], [S60], [S62], [S65]; claim 57

Falling yields during the sell-off are consistent with a flight to quality. The week’s repricing cannot be attributed to rising rates or oil prices on these observations: their high levels set the refinancing backdrop, but their changes ran the other way. The concentration in AI shares and reported AI credit widening favor an AI- and financing-specific interpretation (inference), without identifying causality or excluding the CPI surprise. A daily regression of M1 returns minus S&P equal-weight returns on changes in real ten-year yields, two-year yields and Brent front-month, with CPI/PPI-day dummies, would test that reading. Non-AI duration controls (unprofitable growth, biotech/XBI, REITs and utilities), matched-rating AI/non-AI credit pairs, CoreWeave CDS versus CDX HY, intraday release/headline timing, and breakeven versus real-yield decomposition remain unperformed checks.

### Oil, fiscal supply and the dollar

EIA attributes the oil shock to infrastructure and tanker attacks, notably Saudi Arabia's East–West pipeline bypassing Hormuz. Freight, insurance and inventory depletion amplify the shortage, connecting energy, inflation, rates and sovereign budgets. [S14]

EIA completed its $105/bbl Q4 forecast on October 1, before physical Brent reached $135.51 on October 2. The ten available October spot observations through October 14 average $126.977, rounded to $126.98. With an illustrative 64 observation days in Q4, the remaining 54 would have to average (64×$105 − 10×$126.977)/54 = $100.93, roughly $101 (B). This makes the forecast look low relative to the observed October path, not impossible; it is a conditional calculation, not a revised EIA forecast. [S14], [S59]; claims 15, 56

The requested oil range is a futures quote from FT's indexed table; full access failed and exchange settlement was not retrieved. EIA/FRED physical Brent is higher. The quoted $105.92–109.95 futures closes are above the roughly $101 remaining-quarter sensitivity, but they concern different delivery horizons and dates. Those differences may explain the physical/futures gap; the precise basis remains unverified. [S11], [S12]

EIA estimates Middle East shut-ins fell from 5.8m b/d in August to 4.8m in September and reports partial East–West pipeline resumption on September 22. Recovery is already partly observed, not just forecast; it does not establish normalization. [S14]

Power exposure depends on local generation, PPAs and backup fuel. September gasoline explained over half the monthly CPI rise. Commercial electric power PPI fell 0.6% m/m, but consumer electricity CPI rose 0.2% m/m and 4.4% y/y, qualifying a benign power-price reading. These measure different customers and price concepts; none is a data-center PPA index; uniform oil-to-power cost transmission is unsupported. Weaker spending and dearer funding/construction remain conditional channels, while August real PCE growth provides a demand counterweight. [S4], [S5], [S51]

Deficits imply competition for savings. Net borrowing excludes refinancing included in gross issuance. Treasury's advisory committee recommended unchanged coupon sizes despite possible later increases. [S15], [S16], [S17]

Long yields combine expected short rates and a model-estimated term premium. Fiscal supply, inflation uncertainty and growth can all lift yields. No contemporaneous ACM premium was extracted; fiscal stress cannot explain the entire move by assumption. [S18]

The dollar reading is a reference, not a full-year trend claim. Appreciation raises foreign buyers' local-currency GPU and debt costs; depreciation reverses that burden. Hedges and revenue currencies determine exposure. [S10]

MGX's founders Mubadala and G42 connect sovereign finance with G42's Stargate UAE compute partnership. Avoid counting these related pools repeatedly. AIP includes outside equity and debt. Aligned has closed, but enterprise value includes financing and payments to sellers, rather than solely new capex or MGX capital. [S25], [S26], [S27], [S28]

### Geopolitical tails and scenario transmission

Taiwan disruption threatens physical supply. On October 15, TSMC scheduled Arizona fab two volume production for Q3 2027 and described building 13 leading-edge/advanced-packaging fabs in Taiwan and five to six overseas. Diversification is proceeding, but the disclosed plans do not establish immediate substitutability for Taiwan supply. Its Q3 revenue of $46.72bn and 58.7% operating margin are a profitable upstream counterweight; they do not prove customers' incremental AI returns. [S46], [S52], [S53]

BIS permits case-by-case chip licensing, but Nvidia reports Chinese restrictions and tariffs it cannot pass through. AMD distinguishes uncodified revenue-share expectations from inspection tariffs. Neither is equity or fresh funding. [S42], [S43], [S45]

China’s MOFCOM/MOST Announcement No. 48 of October 16 proposes catalogue changes while imposing immediate temporary pre-export registration for Chinese technology. Its 23 AI categories concern models, agents, compute clusters and information processing; registration includes unfinished outbound transfers, offshore licensing and changes of technology ownership/control to foreign entities. The AI provisions do not specify chips or AI hardware (the separate photovoltaic provisions do mention certain equipment). The direct channel is Chinese labs’ offshore licensing and foreign acquisitions of Chinese technology owners; a small direct effect on US AI capex is an inference, not a measured impact. Public-domain technology is exempt. Registration is already effective; the proposed catalogue is not a final blanket export prohibition. Final thresholds and practical offshore licensing effects remain uncertain; existing open models are not universally banned. [S44]

Russia–Ukraine affects refinery, sanctions and shipping risks. The latest US Russia–Iran sanctions order includes extensive waivers: headline penalties do not establish lost oil supply (primary, A). Middle East escalation threatens oil, LNG routes and Gulf investment. These transmission hypotheses have no measured Russian contribution here. Nvidia reports no significant impact on Israel operations so far. [S49], [S14], [S43]
