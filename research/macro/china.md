# China: supply competitor, demand pool and policy risk

*Last updated: 2026-11-05 · Cross-checked by: Codex gpt-6-astra, Claude Opus 5.5 · Data: [china-hyperscaler-capex.csv](/data/china-hyperscaler-capex.csv)*

## Summary

- China is an evidenced competitor at the cheaper model tier and in conventional DRAM. Chinese labs supplied 69% of OpenRouter tokens while closed weights retained 73% of spend; these are platform shares, not global shares or measured Western margin losses (claims 21–23).
- Alibaba, Tencent and Baidu’s combined June-quarter capex was $19.433B against $171.210B for the US comparison group. Differing definitions limit precision, but the evidence does not establish China as a replacement for US infrastructure demand (claim 1).
- Alibaba, Tencent and Baidu spent beyond operating cash. Alibaba’s cloud/compute adjusted EBITA was +RMB5.628B versus −RMB13.861B in labs/apps. Tencent’s prepayment-adjusted cash-flow figure removes cash already committed (claims 3–4).
- Financing includes Alibaba equity, Tencent’s priced notes, ByteDance’s reported facility and DeepSeek’s prospective round. MiniMax’s 17.9% gross margin coexists with a $293.031M adjusted loss. Shareholders bear immediate dilution and impairment risk; debt extends potential exposure to lenders (claims 6–13).
- DeepSeek’s Ascend software advances substitution, but CXMT’s reported low HBM yields and alleged Huawei customer-sourcing requests indicate unresolved memory constraints. These remain grade C, reported, unverified; shipment plans do not establish usable capacity. Disclosed training runs are not full development budgets (claims 14–19).
- Interim export registration is active; final technology restrictions remain proposed. Reported H200 import blockage and US federal exclusions constrain distinct customer channels. Taiwan remains a systemic supply risk. The biggest uncertainty is whether useful capacity and customer revenue can earn returns on committed capital; the evidence does not establish a China-wide AI bubble (claims 25–29).

## Analysis

China supplies models, infrastructure and domestic semiconductor alternatives while also buying equipment. The capex comparison measures selected groups, not national AI investment. Alibaba, Tencent and Baidu report group capex; the US denominator mixes cash purchases with lease-inclusive measures. Procurement timing, anticipated CPU demand and component inflation also affect Alibaba’s spending. Expenditure therefore need not translate proportionately into delivered compute or realised AI demand (claims 1–2).

Profitable platform operations coexist with investment losses. Alibaba’s cloud/compute segment includes non-AI services; its positive adjusted EBITA does not establish standalone inference margins or investment-cohort returns. AI-product revenue relative to group capex compares different scopes. Tencent’s positive cash-flow counterfactual isolates prepayment timing but does not reduce capital already exposed. The cash shortfalls are quarterly consolidated observations, not normalised annual AI losses (claims 3–5).

Alibaba’s completed placement makes shareholder funding concrete. Tencent’s notes were priced, while ByteDance’s loan and DeepSeek’s prospective round remain reported, unverified; issuance, drawdown and closing must be distinguished. General corporate borrowing cannot all be allocated to AI. Tencent also appears among DeepSeek’s reported investors, broadening exposure beyond its own build. MiniMax’s thin group margin and adjusted loss show why rapid revenue growth can still depend on financing. Pure-play lab investors face dilution and impairment first; platform creditors also depend on profitable core businesses (claims 6–13).

Cheap APIs and open weights create migration options. OpenRouter demonstrates adoption but cannot measure global competition: Chinese origin and weight access overlap, and closed weights do not mean Western origin. Spend share does not establish absolute revenue or margins. Western margins fall only if substitutes meet task-success, latency, reliability and governance requirements and price cuts outrun cost reductions and usage growth. Cheaper tokens can also expand demand for equipment. MiniMax’s group margin does not establish near-cost pricing at every Chinese lab (claims 11–13, 20–22).

Training-run disclosures exclude discovery costs and inherited training. A large proposed serving installation is compatible with efficient training. Released inference software establishes porting progress, while vendor kernel benchmarks leave full-system cost, reliability and deployment unmeasured. Huawei component counts and finished-accelerator plans require reconciliation. CXMT’s reported low yields and prospective limited production leave qualified HBM supply unestablished. Alleged customer-sourced memory could shift procurement cost and execution risk onto accelerator buyers; it does not prove direct Korean-vendor supply to Huawei. Conventional-DRAM share supports a separate competitive channel (claims 14–19, 23).

China is consequently an evidenced margin competitor, with no demonstrated replacement for US infrastructure demand. Nvidia’s reported Hong Kong stockpile is evidence of an import bottleneck, not measured global oversupply; ownership and revenue recognition remain unknown. Equipment makers’ exposure shows that demand effects differ by layer. Interim registration covers unfinished exports and foreign ownership/control changes as well as transfers and licensing. The final catalogue remains proposed; federal exclusion does not erase private adoption. Taiwan interruption could stop supply despite healthy demand, and neither equity prices nor military activity identifies its probability (claims 1, 24–29).

Listed-lab repricing alongside prospective private funding is compatible with different valuations and financing needs. Bloomberg’s attribution to token-price cuts is reporting, not a causal margin estimate. The risk interpretation remains grade C: utilisation, incremental returns and security valuations are insufficient to establish a China-wide bubble (claim 30).

## Open questions

- DeepSeek’s funding estimates remain unreconciled and closing unverified. Closing documents would settle size, terms and cash received (claim 10).
- ByteDance’s disputed budget remains unresolved; audited spending and facility drawdown would clarify exposure (claims 8–9).
- Huawei supply estimates use different units and product mixes. CXMT qualification, achieved yields, accepted deliveries and matched-task costs would test substitution; reported customer-sourcing requests do not establish a reliable HBM channel (claims 16–19).
- H200 stock ownership, clearance and revenue recognition would distinguish an import bottleneck from supplier inventory risk (claim 25).
- Regulator records and the final export catalogue would clarify reported approvals and future releases (claims 27–28).
- Cohort returns, utilisation, depreciation and standalone serving margins would distinguish productive investment from overbuild. Platform shares and group accounts do not settle this.
